Home warranty jobs: are they worth it, and how do you get on?

Slow week, empty schedule, and someone says "just sign up with the warranty companies". Some shops fill their winters that way. Others lose money on every call and hate it. Here's how the work really flows, what it pays, where to apply, and how to tell which one you'd be.

13 min read Checked against the networks' own pages By Wyatt, Biggify

You'll walk away with

  • Where and how to apply to each major network
  • The paperwork list, so you apply once and get approved
  • A 5-minute way to find your true cost per call before you agree to any rate

The 20-second version

Jump to the cost math →
  1. Warranty work is volume at a discount. The company sends the job; you accept their rates, rules and paperwork.
  2. Know your true cost per call first. Drive time, hold time and second trips for parts are where the money goes.
  3. Apply with your papers ready: license, liability certificate naming the company, workers comp or exemption, W-9.
  4. Start with one or two networks, learn how fast they pay, then add more or walk away.
  5. The real prize is the customer. Do great work, get the review, and be the name they call directly next time.

01

How warranty work flows

A home warranty is a service contract a homeowner buys, often thrown in when a house is sold. When something covered breaks, the homeowner doesn't call you. They call the warranty company. Here's what happens next:

  1. The homeowner files a claim and pays a service fee.It's a flat fee per visit set by their plan. Many companies have you collect it at the door, before you diagnose.
  2. The warranty company dispatches a contractor.That's you, through their portal, an app, a phone call, email or (still, with some) fax. You accept or decline.
  3. You go out, diagnose and report back.If the repair is more than a small amount, you call or submit for authorization before you touch anything.
  4. They decide what's covered.Repair, replace, or sometimes offer the homeowner cash instead. Their contract decides, not you.
  5. You do the work and bill the warranty companyat the rates in your agreement, through their portal, with photos and notes. Then you wait to be paid.
Who your customer really is

On a warranty call you have two customers: the homeowner in front of you, and the company paying you. The homeowner judges you on the visit. The company judges you on speed, paperwork and cost. You need both to keep the work coming.

02

The honest pros and cons

✓Why shops do it

  • Steady calls in slow seasons. Water heaters and appliances fail all year.
  • Fills gaps in the schedule. A cancelled job becomes a paid call instead of an empty afternoon.
  • No ad money to get the job. The company found the customer, not you.
  • New customers in your area who may call you directly for the work the warranty won't cover.
  • Keeps new techs busy while they learn, on simpler repairs.

✕What owners complain about

  • Lower rates than your retail price, sometimes far lower.
  • Authorization calls and portal time you don't get paid for.
  • Parts rules: they may want their supplier, their price, or a cheaper part.
  • Slow payment. Some pay in days, some net 45 after they approve the invoice.
  • Frustrated customers. They're angry at the warranty company, and you're the one standing in their kitchen.

The honest test: warranty work makes sense when it fills time you'd otherwise lose, at a rate that still covers your true cost. It hurts when it pushes out a full-price customer, or when you never ran the numbers.

03

Which trades they need

The core of every network is the systems and appliances in a plan:

  • HVAC: heating, cooling, ductwork, thermostats.
  • Plumbing: leaks, stoppages, water heaters, well pumps, septic pumping on some plans.
  • Electrical: panels, wiring, ceiling fans and fixtures.
  • Appliance repair: fridges, ovens, dishwashers, washers and dryers.
  • Garage door openers, roofing leaks, pool and spa equipment, locksmiths, handyman work and more, depending on the company.

Frontdoor's contractor page, for example, lists 17 trades, from appliance techs and HVAC to septic, well pump and pool techs. If your trade is on a plan's coverage list, someone is dispatching that work in your area.

06

Your true cost per call (do this before you agree to any rate)

Most owners compare the warranty rate to their retail price. The right comparison is the warranty rate against what a call actually costs you.

  1. Hours per call.Drive there and back + time on site + time on authorization calls, photos and the portal. Be honest about the hold time.
  2. Loaded labor per hour.The tech's wage plus payroll taxes, workers comp and benefits. If it's you, use what you'd pay a tech.
  3. Truck cost per call.Miles there and back × your cost per mile (fuel, payment, insurance, repairs).
  4. Overhead per hour.Insurance, office, phone, software and so on for a month, divided by the hours your techs bill in a month.
  5. Add the second trip.Out of every 10 calls, how many need a return visit for parts? Add that share of a whole extra trip.

True cost per call = hours × (labor + overhead) + truck cost + share of second trips.

Example only2.5 hours × ($45 labor + $30 overhead) = $187.50. Truck: 30 miles × $0.70 = $21. 3 in 10 calls need a second trip: + about $62. True cost ≈ $270.
Your numberRun your own. These are made-up figures to show the math. If the warranty company pays less than your number on a typical call, every call loses money.

Then look at what you'd really get: the service fee you collect plus what the company pays for a typical diagnosis and repair in your trade. Ask for their rate sheet before you sign. Also ask how long payment takes. Net 45 on a busy month is a lot of your money sitting in someone else's account.

07

Running the call without losing money

  • Call the homeowner fast. Response time is how the networks score you. A dispatch that sits until tonight can go to the next contractor.
  • Collect the service fee first if your agreement says you do. Explain it isn't your fee: "That's the fee your warranty plan charges for the visit."
  • Photograph everything before you start: model and serial plate, the failure, the area around it, any code issues. Photos win authorization arguments.
  • Get authorization before you work, and write down the authorization number, who gave it and the amount. No number, no work.
  • Learn their parts rules before day one: who supplies, what markup is allowed, whether they want a cheaper equivalent.
  • Invoice the same day. Some companies void invoices that come in late.
Say this to a frustrated homeowner

"I get it. I don't work for the warranty company, I'm the local [plumber] they sent. I'll tell you exactly what I find and send it to them today, with photos. What they cover is their call, but I'll make sure they have everything they need to say yes."

That one line moves you to their side of the table. You're not defending the warranty company, and you're not trashing it either.

08

Non-covered work, done honestly

Plans exclude a lot: code upgrades, access work, some parts, pre-existing problems. Homeowners often need that work. Offering it is fine. Pushing it isn't.

✓Do

  • Show in writing what's covered and what isn't, with photos
  • Give a clear price for the non-covered part, separate from the warranty work
  • Get the homeowner's OK (signed or texted) before you start it
  • Follow your agreement's rules for how to quote and bill it

✕Don't

  • Say "your warranty won't cover this" before the company has decided
  • Scare people into a replacement they don't need
  • Start extra work and ask later
  • Bill the homeowner for anything the company already paid for

Warranty companies watch complaints closely. One pushy upsell can cost you the whole account, and the homeowner's review lands on your Google profile, not theirs.

09

Turning warranty customers into your own

The warranty rate is thin. The homeowner who remembers you and calls you directly next time pays full price. That's where warranty work earns its keep.

  1. Read your agreement first.Some contracts limit soliciting the warranty company's members. Know the line before you go near it.
  2. Be the name they remember.Lettered truck, uniform, a business card on the counter with your direct number. Say your company's name, not just "the warranty guy".
  3. Ask for a Google review before you leave.A homeowner you just helped through a frustrating claim is often the warmest review you'll get all week.
  4. Answer when they call you directly.Months later they'll search your name or find your card. If that call goes to voicemail, they call the next name on Google.

Every warranty job is a free shot at a review in a new neighborhood. The free review kit has the words and the text message to ask.

10

When to say no

  • The rate is under your true cost on a typical call, and they won't move.
  • You're already booked with full-price work. A warranty call that bumps a retail customer is a loss, even if it's "paid".
  • The job is outside your zips or your skill. Declining is better than a slow, sloppy visit that hurts your score.
  • Payment keeps slipping, or invoices get "lost" more than once.
  • The agreement has terms you can't live with: long workmanship warranties at their rates, rules on your own customers, or disputes handled far from home.
  • Your crew dreads the calls. Unhappy techs on unhappy-customer jobs is how good people quit.
The healthy way to use it

Use warranty work to fill the slow weeks while you build the calls that come straight to you: a Google profile that ranks, reviews that keep coming, and a phone that always gets answered. As those grow, take fewer warranty calls, and only the ones that pay.

11

Quick answers

Does it cost money to join a warranty network?

None of the contractor pages we checked mention an application fee. Your real cost is the paperwork, the insurance they require, and the lower rates you agree to. If anyone asks for a fee to get you onto a warranty network, find out exactly who they are before paying.

Who collects the service fee?

With many companies you collect the homeowner's service fee at the door, before you diagnose. Your agreement says whether you collect it and how it counts toward what you're paid. Read that part before your first call.

Can I sell non-covered work on a warranty call?

Usually yes, if it's real, the homeowner agrees, and you follow your agreement's rules for quoting it. Show what's covered and what isn't in writing, give a price, and let them say no without pressure.

Can I market to warranty customers afterwards?

Read your agreement first. Some contracts limit soliciting the warranty company's members. Doing great work, leaving your card, asking for a Google review and being the name they remember is usually how warranty customers become your own.

How many networks should I join?

Start with one or two that cover your trade and area, learn their rules and payment speed, then decide. Joining five at once means five sets of rules, portals and rates before you know if any of them pays.
