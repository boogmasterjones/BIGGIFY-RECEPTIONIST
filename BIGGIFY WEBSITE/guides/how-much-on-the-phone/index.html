Phones & pricing

"How much?" on the first call: what to say so price shoppers don't hang up

"It depends" makes them hang up. A bare number gets you compared to the cheapest guy online. There's a middle way that's honest and books more jobs: a few quick questions, a real range, and a clear next step. Here are the words, trade by trade.

14 min read Checked against current rules By Wyatt, Biggify

You'll walk away with

  • The 4-step answer to "how much?" that works in any trade
  • Short copy-paste scripts for 9 trades, plus the 4 hardest push-backs

The 20-second version

Jump to the cheat card →
  1. Acknowledge it. "Good question, I'll give you a real number."
  2. Ask 2 or 3 quick questions. What's going on, how old it is, when they need it.
  3. Give an honest range and say what moves it. Low end if X, high end if Y.
  4. Offer the next step. A visit, photos, or a diagnostic fee that comes off the bill if they go ahead (only if that's how you work).
  5. Never dodge, never blurt. No "it depends" with nothing after it. No bare number with no context.

01

Why "how much?" ends calls

Most people who ask "how much?" aren't cheapskates. They're nervous about getting taken, and price is the only question they know to ask. How you handle the next ten seconds decides whether they book or call the next name on Google.

✕The two answers that lose the job

  • The dodge: "It depends, we'd have to come out and look." They hear "I'm hiding something" and hang up to find someone who'll tell them.
  • The bare number: "$189." No context, so they compare it to the cheapest number they've seen and you lose on price alone. Or the real job costs more and they feel tricked at the door.

✓What works instead

  • Show you heard the question
  • Ask just enough to not be guessing
  • Give a real range and say what moves it
  • Tell them the next step and what it costs

The caller gets an honest answer. You get the details to price it right. And by the time you say the number, you've shown you know the job, which is what they were really checking.

02

The 4-step answer

  1. Acknowledge the question."Good question, and I'll give you a real number. Can I ask you two quick things so I'm not guessing?" Almost nobody says no to that.
  2. Ask 2 or 3 quick questions.What's going on? How old is it (or how big)? When do you need it done? Three questions, not twenty. This isn't the inspection.
  3. Give an honest range, then say what moves it."Most jobs like that run [$low] to [$high]. It's on the low end if [X], higher if [Y]." Say the range out loud, every time.
  4. Offer the next step."The way to get an exact price is [a visit / a couple of photos / a diagnostic]. That's [$fee], and it comes off the bill if you go ahead." Only say the last part if that's really your policy.

Say their answers back

"So it's a 12-year-old gas unit and it stopped heating last night." One sentence. It proves you listened, and it makes the range that follows sound like it's about their job, not a menu price.

03

Get your ranges ready first (20 minutes, once)

The scripts below have blanks like [$low] and [$high] on purpose. Prices change a lot from town to town and from shop to shop, and a range you made up on the spot is worse than none. Fill in your own real numbers once, and every call gets easier.

  1. Pick your 5 most-asked jobs.The ones people call about most: "AC not cooling", "replace water heater", "mow my yard".
  2. Pull your last 10 to 20 invoices for each.From your invoicing app, your job software, or the paper pile.
  3. Drop the strangest high one and the strangest low one.What's left is your honest range.
  4. Write down what made jobs land high.Attic install, extra parts, permits, removal, two stories. Those are your "what moves it" lines.
  5. Tape it next to the phone and share it with whoever answers.Your spouse, your office person, your answering service. Everyone gives the same range.
Don't lowball the bottom of the range

If almost no real job lands at your low number, don't say it. People remember the first number they hear. A range you hit is worth more than a cheap-sounding one you don't.

04

Scripts by trade (copy, fill in, use today)

Each one is the same 4 steps. Swap in your numbers and change the words until they sound like you. If you don't charge a diagnostic or trip fee, drop that line and offer the free visit or photos instead.

Plumbing (leak or clog)

Script

"Good question, I'll give you a real number. Is it a leak or a clog? Which fixture, or is it more than one drain? And is water coming out anywhere right now?

OK. A clog like that usually runs [$low] to [$high]. Low end if it clears at the fixture. Higher if it's down in the main line or we need a camera to find it. We charge [$fee] to come out and find the cause, and that comes off the bill if you have us fix it."

HVAC (not cooling or heating)

Script

"I'll give you a straight answer. What's it doing: blowing warm, not turning on, or freezing up? About how old is the system? And is anyone in the house who can't take the heat or cold?

Most repairs on a system that age land between [$low] and [$high]. It depends on the part: a capacitor or a clogged drain line is the low end, a motor or a refrigerant leak is higher. If it's a replacement conversation, I'll tell you that straight, with numbers. The diagnostic is [$fee] and it's credited if we do the repair."

Electrical

Script

"Sure, let me ask two quick things so I don't guess wrong. What do you need done, and is anything sparking, hot or smelling like burning right now? About how old is the house?

A job like that typically runs [$low] to [$high]. What moves it: how easy it is to get wire to that spot, whether your panel has room, and whether it needs a permit. I can come look and give you an exact, written price. That visit is [$fee / free]."

If anything is sparking, hot or smells like burning: tell them to shut off that breaker and treat it as urgent, before any talk about price.

Water heaters

Script

"Good question. Tank or tankless, gas or electric? Do you know how many gallons or how old it is? There's usually a sticker on the side. And is it leaking right now?

Replacing a [40/50-gallon gas] tank, installed and the old one hauled away, is usually [$low] to [$high]. What moves it is where it sits (garage is easiest, attic or closet costs more) and whatever your local code needs, like a pan, an expansion tank or venting changes. If you can text me a photo of it and the sticker, I can usually give you a firm price before we come out."

Roofing

Script

"Happy to give you real numbers. Is this a leak, or are you thinking about a full roof? What's on it now (shingle, tile, metal) and about how old is it? One story or two?

A leak repair like that is usually [$low] to [$high]. A full replacement on a house that size with [shingles] typically runs [$low] to [$high]. What moves it is the size, how steep it is, and whether there's rotten wood underneath, which nobody can see from the ground. The inspection is free and I'll measure it so the price is exact, not a guess."

Tile and flooring

Script

"Good question. Which rooms, and about how many square feet, or the room sizes? What's down there now? And have you picked the tile or flooring yet?

For that, labor usually runs [$low] to [$high] per square foot, plus materials. What moves it: tearing out the old floor, how level the floor underneath is, and the pattern (a big plain layout is quicker than a herringbone). I'll come measure and check the floor, and you'll get one written price with everything in it."

Pressure washing

Script

"Sure. What are we cleaning: house, driveway, roof, pool deck? One story or two? And any rust, oil or paint stains you want gone?

A [one-story house wash] is usually [$low] to [$high]. Size moves it most. Roofs are priced on their own, and rust or oil stains are extra because they need a different treatment. Text me your address and a couple of photos and I'll send a firm price today."

Lawn care

Script

"Good question. What's the address so I can look at the yard size? Weekly, every other week, or a one-time cleanup? And how long has it been since the last cut?

For a yard around that size, a regular cut is usually [$low] to [$high] per visit. The first cut costs more if it's overgrown. Gates, slopes and bagging clippings can move it too. I'll look at it on the map and text you an exact per-visit price."

Pest control

Script

"Sure. What are you seeing, and where in the house? How long has it been going on? Any kids or pets I should know about?

For general pests in a home that size, the first treatment is usually [$low] to [$high], and ongoing service is [$amount] per [month / quarter] if you want it. What moves it is the pest and how far it's spread. Termites and bed bugs are a different job with different pricing. If it's one of those, the next step is an inspection so I can give you an exact number."

05

The 3 push-backs you'll hear

"Google says it's $50"

That number came from somewhere: a national average, a big-box store's install price, an AI answer, or the price of the part alone. Don't argue with it, and don't call them wrong. Explain what's in yours.

Say this

"Yeah, you'll see numbers like that online. They're usually the part on its own, or an average from all over the country. Ours is [$low] to [$high] here, and that includes [the trip, the labor, haul-away, and our [X]-day warranty on the work]. If you find someone who'll do all of that for $50, honestly, take it."

Only list what you really include. If you don't warranty labor, don't say it.

"The other guy quoted less"

  1. Don't knock them."They might be a good choice." Owners who trash competitors sound desperate.
  2. Ask what's in their price."Do you mind if I ask what's included? Permit, haul-away, the same size and brand, warranty on the labor?"
  3. Point out the real gaps, plainly."Ours includes the permit and the expansion tank your code requires. If theirs doesn't, those get added on the day."
  4. If it's truly the same job for less, say so."If it's the same job, they're cheaper, and that's fair. If anything changes, call me." That honesty is remembered.
Don't drop your price just because they asked

If you cut $200 the moment someone pushes, the next question is "so what was the first price?" If you want to lower it, take something out of the job (a cheaper model, they haul the old one away, a later date) so the price still means something.

"Just give me a ballpark"

Give one. Refusing is the dodge, and it's what makes people hang up. Give the whole honest range, say what puts a job at each end, and then ask one question to narrow it.

Say this

"Sure. Jobs like that run anywhere from [$low] to [$high]. The cheap end is [simple version], the top end is [the hard version]. Can I ask one thing so I can tell you which end you're probably at?"

06

When you'll be more than they expect

Sometimes you can tell from the questions that the real number is going to hurt: a full system, a whole roof, a panel upgrade. Bad news gets worse with time. Say it on the phone, not at the door.

  1. Warn them before the number."I want to be straight with you, because this one's a bigger job than most people expect."
  2. Say the range plainly.Don't mumble it or bury it in a long story.
  3. Give the one-line reason."Because it's in the attic and code now needs [X]." People accept a price they understand.
  4. Offer real options if you have them.Repair now vs replace, a standard model vs a premium one, doing it in two phases. Mention financing only if you actually offer it.
  5. Let them think."No pressure. Want me to text you the range and what's included so you can look it over?" Then follow up once, a few days later.

✓Honest ways to help them say yes

  • Options at different prices
  • A written price with what's included
  • Time to think, and one friendly follow-up

✕Skip these

  • "This price is only good today" when it isn't
  • Scaring them with a danger that isn't real
  • A low phone number, then a big one at the door
07

When to politely say "we're not the right fit"

Not every caller is your customer, and that's fine. Spending an hour driving out to someone who was never going to pay your price costs you a real job. It's usually time to say so when:

  • Their budget is far under your honest low end, even after you've offered a cheaper option.
  • It's a job you don't do, or don't do well.
  • They're outside the area you actually serve.
  • They want something you won't do, like skipping a permit or installing parts you can't stand behind.
  • They only want a number to beat someone else up with, and say so.
Say this

"I appreciate you calling. Honestly, at that budget I don't think we're the right fit, and I'd hate to waste your time. [A handyman / a company that does just that / the supply house] might be a better match. If anything changes, you've got my number."

Kind, quick and honest. They hang up without a bad taste, and some of them call back later for something you do want.

08

Voicemail and text versions

Plenty of "how much?" questions arrive as a voicemail, a missed call or a text. Same 4 steps, shorter. Answer fast: the longer you wait, the more likely they've already booked someone else.

Calling back and getting their voicemail

Leave this

"Hi, it's [name] from [business], returning your call about the [job]. Quick answer on price: jobs like that usually run [$low] to [$high], depending on [the main thing]. Call or text me back at [number] with [one detail, like the age or size] and I'll tighten that up for you. Thanks."

Text reply to "how much for…?"

Text 1 (right away)

"Hi, [name] from [business] here. Happy to give you a real price. Quick questions: [question 1]? [question 2]? A photo helps a lot if you can send one."

Text 2 (after they answer)

"Thanks! For that, it's usually [$low]–[$high]. Low end if [X], higher if [Y]. To get an exact price, [we can come by [day] / it's a [$fee] diagnostic, taken off the bill if you go ahead]. Want me to set that up?"

Keep texts to people who reached out

Replying to someone who called or texted you about a job is normal. Adding them to promo texts later is a different thing with its own consent rules. If they say stop, stop.

10

Quick answers

Should I give prices over the phone at all?

Give a range, not a dodge and not a bare number. Ask 2 or 3 quick questions first, then give an honest range, say what moves it up or down, and offer the next step to get an exact price.

What if they just want a ballpark?

Give one. Say the wide range, what puts a job at the low end and what puts it at the high end, then ask one question to narrow it. Refusing to give any number is what makes people hang up.

Should I charge a diagnostic or trip fee?

That's your call. If you do, say the amount up front and, if it's your policy, say it comes off the bill when they go ahead. Never spring a fee at the door that wasn't mentioned on the phone.

What do I say when another company quoted less?

Don't knock the other company. Ask what their price includes, compare the same job line by line, and explain what's in yours. If it really is the same job for less, say so honestly.

Should my prices be on my website?

Ranges for your common jobs, with what moves them, can help the same way they do on the phone: people arrive already knowing roughly what to expect. Keep them current, and only post ranges you really land in.
