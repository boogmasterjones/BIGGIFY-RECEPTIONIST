Google reviews

Get more Google reviews without tripping Google's filters (or breaking FTC rules)

Your customers are happy. They say so on the driveway. Then they never post a word. That's not ingratitude. It's friction and timing. Here's the 30-second ask that fixes it, the habits that get honest reviews filtered, and the rules that can cost you more than a review.

15 min read Checked against current rules By Wyatt, Biggify

You'll walk away with

  • Word-for-word ask scripts for the driveway and for a text
  • The 6 habits that get real reviews filtered, and what to do instead
  • A one-month plan you can start on your next job

The 20-second version

Jump to the one-month plan →
  1. Ask every customer, right after the job. Not just the happy ones. In person first, then a text with your direct link within the hour.
  2. Let them review on their own phone, later. Never on your tablet, your phone or your Wi-Fi.
  3. No rewards. None. No discounts, drawings or gift cards, not even for "honest" reviews.
  4. Don't tell them what to write, and don't ask them to name your tech.
  5. Steady beats bursts. A few a week from real jobs, every week, is what sticks.

01

Why happy customers don't leave reviews

It's almost never because they didn't like the work. It's two things you control: how hard it is, and when you ask.

Friction"Search us on Google and leave a review" is five steps and a login. Most people quit at step two.
TimingThe water's hot again tonight. By next week they've forgotten how bad it was, and you.
  • They don't know how. Plenty of people have never written a Google review. Finding the right business, the right button and the stars takes effort.
  • Nobody asked them directly. "Reviews help us!" printed on an invoice isn't an ask. A person saying "would you do me a favor?" is.
  • The moment passed. Relief and gratitude are highest the hour the job's done. That's when to ask.
  • They think you don't need it. Busy trucks look like they're doing fine. Tell them it really helps a small business.

Do this today: pull up your last 10 finished jobs. How many got a clear ask with a link? That number is your real review problem.

02

The 30-second ask, in person and by text

The ask has two parts. You (or your tech) say it out loud at the end of the job. Then a text with the link lands while they're still pleased. Saying it first makes the text expected, not spam.

For the full walk-through, including what to say when a customer hesitates, see The 30-Second Review Ask.

In person, at the end of the job

Script 1: The driveway ask

"Before I go, can I ask a small favor? We're a local business and Google reviews are how new customers find us. I'll text you a link in a few minutes. If you've got a minute later, an honest review would really help."

Script 2: When they've already said thanks

"Thank you, that means a lot. Would you mind saying that on Google? I'll text you the link so it only takes a minute."

By text, within the hour

Script 3: The follow-up text

"Hi [first name], this is [your name] from [business]. Thanks for having us out today. If you have a minute, an honest Google review would really help us: [your review link]. Reply STOP to opt out."

Script 4: One reminder, 3 to 5 days later, then stop

"Hi [first name], [your name] from [business] again. No pressure at all. If you get a chance, here's that review link: [your review link]. Thanks either way!"

✓Keep in the ask

  • The word "honest"
  • Their first name and your business name
  • The direct link, nothing else to click
  • A way to opt out of texts

✕Leave out

  • "5-star", "positive" or "good" review
  • "Mention Mike" or any staff name
  • Words to copy, keywords or your town name
  • Anything they get in return

Vary the wording a little between your techs and over time. You don't need 20 versions, but you don't want every text identical forever either.

03

Your direct review link and QR code

The direct link opens straight to the stars on your profile. No searching, no picking the wrong "Smith Plumbing". It's the single biggest friction cut you can make.

  1. Get the link.Sign in to the Google account that manages your profile, search your business name on Google, and look for Ask for reviews (on some screens it's under Read reviews or Get more reviews). Google gives you a short link and a QR code.
  2. Test it.Open the link on your phone in a private window. It should land on your review box, not a search page.
  3. Save it everywhere your team texts from.A phone contact named "Review link", a text shortcut, your job software's templates.
  4. Put the QR on things customers keep.A card left on the counter, the invoice, the paperwork folder. Things they look at later, at home.
Use Google's link, not a redirect

Some review tools wrap your link in their own "smart" link that asks how the visit went first. If the unhappy path doesn't also lead to Google, that's gating (see section 07). Google's own link is free and clean.

04

Timing: right after the job

WhenWhatWhy
Job done, before you leaveThe spoken ask (Script 1 or 2)Gratitude is highest. A face asking beats any text.
Within the hourThe text with the link (Script 3)They're home, phone in hand, still pleased.
3 to 5 days laterOne reminder (Script 4) if no review yetMany reviews come from the reminder. One is plenty.
After thatStopMore asks annoy good customers and can breach texting limits.

Multi-day jobs (a remodel, a roof, a new system): ask at the final walk-through, not on day one. Callbacks and warranty visits: ask after the problem's fixed, the same way. Every customer gets asked, whatever you think they'll say.

Texting hours: send between 9 am and 8 pm in the customer's time zone. Federal rules allow 8 am to 9 pm, but some states are stricter, so the narrower window is the safe habit.

05

Steady pace beats bursts

Google's rules name "unusual volumes or patterns" of reviews as a warning sign. A burst is exactly that: nothing for months, then 30 in a weekend.

SteadyA few reviews every week, in step with how many jobs you finish. That's what a real business looks like.
BurstsA big "review drive", a blast to your whole customer list, or a contest among techs. Easy for the filter to doubt.
  • Ask every finished job, every week. Pace takes care of itself.
  • Old customers? Don't blast your whole list in one day. If you have permission to text or email them, spread it out over several weeks, a small batch at a time.
  • No quotas for techs. Google's rules now name review quotas for staff as a problem. Reward the habit of asking, not the number of reviews.
  • Recency matters to customers. A profile whose newest review is 8 months old looks closed. Fresh reviews every week look busy.

06

What gets real reviews filtered

Google's filter is a machine. It can't tell your real customer from a fake one, so it looks at patterns. Google doesn't publish exactly how it works. These are patterns its rules name, plus what owners and local search pros see over and over.

HabitWhy it looks fakeDo this instead
BurstsMany reviews in a short window after a quiet stretch.Ask every job, every week. See pace.
Same deviceCustomers typing on your tablet or phone looks like one person with many accounts.Text them the link. They use their own phone.
Same Wi-FiLots of reviews from your office or shop connection.Never offer your Wi-Fi "so it loads faster".
QR scanned on the spot on your own tabletSame device, same place, while you watch. Google says not to require or pressure reviews on the premises.Leave a card behind. They scan it later, at home.
Brand-new accountsA Google account with no photo, no history and one review is easier to doubt.Nothing to fix. Ask everyone, and most of your reviewers will have history.
Links in the reviewLinks, emails and phone numbers look like ads. Google's rules ban them.Never ask customers to add your website or number.
"Get the review before you leave the house"

Lots of coaches teach this. Asking out loud on the spot is fine. Handing over your device, or standing there waiting while they type, is the part that gets reviews filtered and leans on Google's "no pressure on the premises" line.

07

What's against Google's rules

Filtering hides reviews by accident. These get reviews removed on purpose, and the worst of them can put a warning on your whole profile.

✓Allowed

  • Asking every customer for an honest review
  • Sending your direct link by text or email
  • A card or QR code they take home
  • A customer naming your tech on their own
  • Replying to every review, good or bad

✕Gets reviews removed

  • Incentives: payment, discounts, free service, raffle entries, for any review
  • Gating: asking only happy customers, or discouraging bad reviews
  • Specific content: having staff ask for reviews that say certain things
  • Quotas: requiring staff to get a set number of reviews
  • Conflict of interest: you, your staff, ex-staff, family
  • Bought reviews, review swaps, or reviews from many accounts

Incentives, even "for an honest review"

Google bans offering anything in exchange for posting a review: payment, discounts, free goods or services. It doesn't matter whether you ask for an honest one or a 5-star one. That rules out "10% off your next visit", "enter our gift card drawing" and "free filter change for a review".

Review gating

Gating is "Happy? Leave us a Google review. Unhappy? Tell us first." Google's rules say you can't selectively ask for positive reviews or discourage negative ones. That includes software that asks "How did we do?" and sends only 4 and 5 stars to Google. If your tool does that, turn it off. It's fine to give every customer a way to reach you as well as the Google link.

Specific content and staff names

Google's current wording says merchants shouldn't request that staff solicit reviews that include specific content, or a certain number of reviews. That's widely read as covering "please mention Mike" asks and tech quotas. Don't put a name, a service or a town in your ask.

Employees reviewing

Google lists current or former employment as a conflict of interest. Your techs, office staff, ex-employees and family shouldn't review you, even if they really are customers too.

Buying reviews

Sellers of "real reviews from real accounts" are selling fake engagement. When Google catches it, its rules say it can block new reviews for a time, hide existing ones, and show shoppers a warning that fake reviews were removed. Your real reviews disappear with the bought ones.

08

The FTC review rule, in plain English

Google's rules cost you reviews. This one is federal law. The FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials rule (16 CFR Part 465) applies to businesses of every size, and the FTC can seek civil penalties of more than $50,000 per violation. It has already sent warning letters to businesses.

The rule bansWhat it means for a trade business
Fake reviewsWriting, buying or posting reviews from people who aren't real customers, or that misstate their experience. AI-written fake reviews count.
Paying for a sentimentOffering anything in return for a review that has to be positive (or negative about a competitor), said outright or implied.
Undisclosed insider reviewsYou or a manager reviewing your own business without saying who you are. Managers asking staff or relatives for reviews without telling them to disclose the connection. A general ask to all customers isn't covered by this part.
Company-controlled "review" sitesA "best plumbers in town" site you secretly run that ranks you first.
Review suppressionGroundless legal threats, physical threats, intimidation, or knowingly false public accusations to stop or remove a review. Also, showing reviews on your site as if they're all of them while hiding the bad ones.
Fake social proofBuying fake followers or views to look bigger, when you know they're fake and use them for business.
The two rulebooks don't match exactly

The FTC rule targets rewards tied to a positive review. Google bans rewards for any review. Follow Google's stricter line and you're clear of both.

This is a plain-English summary, not legal advice. If you run review contests, pay a review company, or got a letter, talk to a lawyer.

09

Texting rules for review asks

Texting is the best way to deliver the link. It's also where owners trip over rules they didn't know existed.

Consent

  • Get permission to text at booking. "Is it OK if we text you about your appointment and follow up after the job?" Note the yes in your job records.
  • Say who you are in every text, and give a way out ("Reply STOP to opt out").
  • Honor STOP right away, and any plain-English version of it ("stop texting me", "no more").
  • One ask and one reminder per job. Some states limit how many texts you can send on one topic in a day, and penalties under federal and state texting laws are per message.

10DLC: registration for business texting

If your texts go out from software (job management, CRM, a review tool) on a normal 10-digit local number, US carriers require that number to be registered for business texting. That's called 10DLC. Unregistered business texts get filtered or blocked, often without you knowing.

  1. Ask your texting provider"Is my number registered for 10DLC, and is my campaign approved?" You want a clear yes.
  2. Register the business, not just the number.Your legal name and EIN must match IRS records exactly. A mismatch is the most common reason registration fails.
  3. Have a public opt-in and privacy pageon your website that says what you text about and how to opt out. Carriers check for it.
  4. Send a testto your own phone and a friend's on a different carrier.
Texting from your own cell phone

A tech typing one text by hand to the customer they just served is the simplest case. Blasting your whole contact list from a personal phone isn't, and carriers can flag the number. Rules here vary by state; when in doubt, ask a lawyer.

10

What to do with the reviews

Reply to every one

  • Write as the business, in first person. "Thanks, [first name], we're glad the new heater's keeping up", not "The team at ABC Plumbing thanks you."
  • Reply within a few days, good and bad. Future customers read your replies as closely as the reviews.
  • Mention the job naturally when the review does ("glad the drain's behaving"). Don't stuff in towns and keywords.
  • Bad review? Calm, short, take it offline. Never share their job details or argue. Fake ones are a different job: see Hit with fake 1-star reviews.
Reply template: a happy review

"Thank you, [first name]! We're glad [the thing they mentioned] is sorted. We appreciate you trusting us with it, and we're here whenever you need us again."

Reply template: a fair complaint

"Thanks for telling us, [first name]. That's not the experience we want anyone to have. I'd like to hear more and make it right. Please call me at the office and ask for [your name]."

Use them on your website

  • Quote real reviews word for word, with the reviewer's first name and last initial. Don't fix their spelling into a sales pitch.
  • Put them near the call button and on the service page the review is about. A water heater review belongs on the water heater page.
  • Link to your full Google profile ("See all our reviews on Google") so you're never presenting a hand-picked few as the whole picture. That's the FTC's suppression rule.
  • Your star rating and count can go on the site if they're true and you keep them current.
11

Your one-month review plan

Thirty days, about ten minutes a day once it's set up. By the end, asking is a habit, not a campaign.

  1. Week 1: Set up.Get your direct link and QR code and test them. Save the link on every phone that texts customers. Turn off any "How did we do?" screen in your review tool. Ask your texting provider about 10DLC. Pick your scripts from section 02.
  2. Week 2: Start asking every job.Every tech says the driveway ask. The text goes out within the hour. Leave a card with the QR on every job. No exceptions for "difficult" customers.
  3. Week 3: Reminders and replies.Send the one reminder to anyone from week 2 who hasn't reviewed. Reply to every new review within a few days, in first person.
  4. Week 4: Check and adjust.Count jobs finished against asks sent against reviews posted. Look at your profile logged out to confirm the new reviews are showing. Put the best ones on your website.

12

Quick answers

Can I offer a discount for a review?

No. Google's rules ban incentives of any kind for posting a review, including discounts, free service and gift card drawings, even when you ask for an honest review. Offering a reward only for positive reviews also breaks the FTC Consumer Review rule.

Can I ask customers to mention my tech by name?

Don't. Google's rules say businesses shouldn't have staff ask for reviews that include specific content, and that's widely read to cover asking for a tech's name. A customer who names your tech on their own is fine.

Is it OK to have them review on my tablet before I leave?

No. Google says businesses should not require or pressure customers to review while on the premises, and many reviews from one device or one Wi-Fi network look like one person with many accounts. Send the link so they review on their own phone, later.

Can my employees leave us a review?

No. Google treats current or former employment as a conflict of interest, and those reviews get removed. Under the FTC rule, an officer or manager who reviews their own business without disclosing it is breaking the rule.

How many reviews should I ask for each week?

Don't aim for a number. Ask every customer after every finished job. That gives you a steady pace that matches your real workload, which is what a normal business looks like to Google.
