Who owns your stuff? The 15-minute ownership check

Your website, your Google profile, the number on your truck. You paid for all of it. But if your marketing company disappeared tomorrow, could you log in to any of it? Plenty of owners only find out when they try to leave. Here's how to check every piece in about 15 minutes, what the red flags look like, and how to get each one back.

14 min read Checked against current rules By Wyatt, Biggify

You'll walk away with

  • A 2-minute check for each of the 9 things you should own
  • The exact menus to click, and the red flags to look for
  • Contract wording to ask for, and a safe way to leave an agency

The 20-second version

Jump to the checklist →
  1. "You own it" means you can log in and remove everyone else. Not "they'll give it to you if you ask".
  2. Start with your email. Whoever controls the email on an account can reset its password and take it over.
  3. Your domain and phone number matter most. Lose those and customers can't find you, even with a new website.
  4. Your marketing company should be a guest, not the owner. Manager on Google, partner access on Facebook, a user on your ads account.
  5. Fix it while you're on good terms. It's a five-minute favor today and a fight on the day you cancel.

The 15-minute map

Every business that's been online for a few years has the same nine pieces. Grab a coffee and a notepad, and write down for each one: where it lives, whose name is on it, and whether you can log in today.

AssetIf you lose itCheck time
Domain nameYour website and email go dark. Someone else can buy the name.2 min
WebsiteYou start over from a blank page.2 min
Google Business ProfileYour reviews and Maps calls are in someone else's hands.1 min
Analytics, Search Console, AdsYears of data, gone. Ads restart from scratch.3 min
Phone and tracking numbersThe number on your truck rings someone else.2 min
Facebook and InstagramFollowers, reviews and ad history stuck behind someone else's login.2 min
Email at your domainSomeone else can read your mail and reset your passwords.1 min
Yelp, BBB, AngiYou can't answer reviews or fix your details.1 min
Photos and written contentYou may not be allowed to reuse them.1 min
The one-line test

For each one, ask: "If I fired everyone who helps me tomorrow, could I log in and remove them?" If the answer is no, or "I'd have to ask them", you don't own it yet.

02

The master key: your login email

Almost every account on this list is tied to an email address. Whoever controls that email can click "Forgot password" and take the account. So before anything else:

  • Pick one business email that only you control (or you and a partner). Use it as the owner on everything.
  • Turn on 2-step verification on that email, tied to your cell phone, not an employee's or the agency's.
  • Write down the recovery phone and recovery email on that account. If either one belongs to someone else, change it today.
Red flag

An account set up "for you" on an email like yourbusiness.agencyname@gmail.com, or on your old office manager's personal email. Your name is on it, but someone else holds the key.

03

Your domain name (yourbusiness.com)

This is the most important thing on the list. Your website, your email and your Google verification can all hang off it. Losing a domain is the one mistake that's hard to undo.

What "you own it" looks like

  • It sits in a registrar account (GoDaddy, Namecheap, Cloudflare, Squarespace Domains and so on) that you log in to.
  • The registrant (owner) name is you or your company, with your email.
  • Auto-renew is on and the card on file is yours and not expired.

Check it in 2 minutes

  1. Look it up.Go to lookup.icann.org and type your domain. Write down the registrar and the expiry date. The owner's name is often hidden by privacy protection, and that's normal.
  2. Log in to that registrar.If you can't, or you don't know which login, you don't control it yet.
  3. Open the domain's contact details.The registrant should be you or your company, with an email you control.
  4. Check auto-renew and the card.An expired card is how good domains get lost by accident.

✓Good signs

  • It's in your account, under your name
  • Auto-renew on, several years paid ahead if you like
  • Your agency only has access to change website settings, if anything

✕Red flags

  • You get a renewal bill from your agency, not the registrar
  • The registrant name is the agency or a past web designer
  • "Don't worry, we handle all that"

How to get it back

  1. Make a registrar account in your own nameat the same registrar if you can. That's the easiest move.
  2. Ask in writing:"Please move yourbusiness.com into my account at [registrar], or send me the transfer code and unlock it." Same-registrar moves are often called an account change or a "push".
  3. Expect a wait if it was just moved or registered.Registrars commonly block transfers to another registrar for up to 60 days after a registration or transfer. Moving it within the same registrar usually isn't affected.
  4. If they refuse and you're the listed registrant,contact the registrar's support. ICANN, which sets the rules for registrars, takes complaints when a transfer is wrongly blocked.
Don't ignore those "renew your domain" letters, but check them

Some are real registrar notices. Others are look-alike invoices from companies trying to move your domain to them at a higher price. Only ever renew from inside your own registrar account.

04

Your website

There are two kinds of website deals, and owners often don't know which one they have:

DealWhat you really haveWhen you leave
Built on a site builder or the agency's own platformA rented site. You pay monthly to keep it up.Usually your words and photos, not the design. Most builders don't let you move the site to another host.
Built as files (code or WordPress) on hostingA set of files that can live anywhere.The whole site, if the hosting account or code is in your name or handed over.

Neither is wrong. A rented site can be a fair deal. The problem is not knowing which one you're paying for.

Check it in 2 minutes

  • Ask who hosts it and whose account it's in. Can you log in to that account yourself?
  • Ask the one question that matters: "If I leave, what exactly do I get? The files, a copy of the site, or only my text and photos?" Get the answer by email.
  • Read the contract for "license", "proprietary platform" or "site remains the property of". Those mean rented.

Save your stuff today, whatever the answer

  1. Copy every page's textinto a document. Ten minutes, and it's the hardest part to redo.
  2. Download your photosin full size, not screenshots.
  3. Save the list of page addresses(yourbusiness.com/water-heaters and so on). If you ever rebuild, keeping those addresses, or pointing old ones to new ones with redirects, protects the Google rankings you've built.
05

Your Google Business Profile

Your reviews live here. A profile that the agency owns means your reviews are, in practice, theirs.

RoleWho should have itWhat it can do
Primary OwnerYou, on your business emailEverything, including adding and removing people and deleting the profile. There's only one.
OwnerA business partner, if you have oneFull control, including removing users.
ManagerYour marketing company, office managerDay-to-day edits. Can't add or remove people or delete the profile.

Check it in 1 minute

Go to business.google.com signed in with your business email, open your profile, then More → Business Profile settings → People and access. Look for your name with "Primary owner" next to it. Remove anyone you don't recognize or don't work with anymore.

W
How we set it upWyatt, Biggify

Our clients are the Primary Owner of their own Google profiles, and we're added as a Manager. We can do the work, and the client can remove us any day they want. That's the setup you should expect from anyone you hire.

How to get it back

  1. Ask first, in writing:"Please add [your email] as Owner, then transfer Primary Ownership to me." Only the current Primary Owner can hand that role over.
  2. Mind the 7-day wait.Google won't let a brand-new owner become Primary Owner, or remove other users, for the first 7 days. That's normal.
  3. If they won't answer,go to business.google.com/add, search your business and choose Request access. If your address is hidden (you drive to customers), that route may not show up. Contact Google Business Profile support and ask for an ownership transfer.
  4. Never start a new profile to get around it.You lose every review. Our guide to changing your Google profile safely covers this in more detail.
06

Google Analytics, Search Console, Google Ads and LSA

These hold your history: who visited, what they searched, what you spent and what it brought in. Agencies often set them up under their own account. When you leave, the history stays with them.

AccountWhere to checkYou should be
Google AnalyticsAdmin (gear, bottom left) → Account access managementAdministrator at the account level, not only the property.
Search ConsoleSettings → Users and permissionsOwner. If you can't even see this page, you're not one.
Google AdsAdmin → Access and security, then BillingAdmin, and the payments profile should be in your business name with your card.
Local Services AdsInside the Google Ads account it's linked toAdmin on that Google Ads account, same as above.

✓Healthy

  • Your ads account, with the agency linked in as a manager
  • Your card on the billing
  • The agency as a user you can remove

✕Red flags

  • "We run it from our account and bill you"
  • You've never seen the ads dashboard
  • Reports come only as PDFs from them

How to get them back

  • Ask to be added as Administrator or Admin on each one, on your business email. Once you are, you can remove others.
  • Search Console: if your agency verified it with a code on your website or domain, you can verify yourself the same way once you control the domain. When you remove a verified owner, also remove their old verification codes, or they can verify themselves back in.
  • Google Ads: if the ads account was created under the agency's own manager account, ask them to give you Admin access on it before you part ways. If the billing is theirs, ask for your past spend and results history exported first.
07

Phone and tracking numbers

The number on your truck, your yard signs and your Google profile is worth more than your website. Customers save it in their phones for years.

Here's the catch. A tracking number from a marketing company, or a line from a phone service your agency signed you up for, belongs to whoever's name is on the phone account. To move (port) a number to a new phone company, you have to be the account holder.

Check it in 2 minutes

  • List every number on your Google profile, website, Facebook, truck and ads.
  • For each one, ask: whose account is it on? Do I get a bill for it with my name on it?
  • Call your phone company and ask for the account number and a port-out PIN. If they can give them to you, you're the account holder.
Red flag

A tracking number is the main phone on your Google profile, it's not in your name, and the contract says nothing about handing it over. If you leave, that number can be given to another business, and your old customers will call them.

How to keep your number when you leave

  1. Get the promise in writing first:"Any phone number used for my business will be ported to me on request."
  2. Open an account with your new phone companyand ask them to start the port. They'll need the account number, PIN and the account holder's name.
  3. Don't cancel the old service until the port is done.Cancelling first can release the number for good.
  4. If the number can't be moved,put your own number on Google as the main phone and keep the old one as an additional phone while it still works. Then update your website, Facebook and directories the same week.
08

Facebook and Instagram

What "you own it" looks like

  • You have full control of your Facebook Page from your own personal Facebook profile. Meta's own help pages warn that full control lets someone remove anyone, including you, so only people you'd trust with your business get it.
  • Your agency has partial control or task access, or works through their business account as a partner. Either way, you can take it away.
  • Your Instagram login uses your email and your phone.

Check it in 2 minutes

  1. Facebook Page:switch into your Page, then Settings → Page setup → Page access. Your name should be listed with full control. Note everyone else.
  2. Meta Business Suite(business.facebook.com), if you use one: open Settings and find your Page and Instagram under the business's assets. They should belong to your business, not show up as shared from a partner.
  3. Instagram:in the app, open your profile, the menu, then Accounts Center → Personal details. The email and phone should be yours.

How to get it back

  • Ask whoever has full control to give you full control. Once you have it, you can lower or remove their access.
  • A Page with no one holding full control can end up deactivated, so never remove yourself to "clean up".
  • For Instagram, ask for the login, then change the password, the email, the phone and turn on 2-step verification, in that order, the same day.
09

Email at your domain (you@yourbusiness.com)

If your work email runs through Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 and someone else holds the admin login, they can read your mail, reset your password and lock you out. Since that email probably owns your other accounts, this is a big one.

  • Google Workspace: go to admin.google.com. If you can get in and you're a Super Admin, you own it.
  • Microsoft 365: go to admin.microsoft.com. You want to be a Global Administrator.
  • Can't get in? Ask whoever set it up to make you a Super Admin or Global Administrator, then check the billing is in your name.
  • The fallback: because the email runs on your domain, whoever controls the domain can usually prove ownership to Google or Microsoft. Another reason the domain comes first.
10

Yelp, BBB, Angi and other review sites

Your reviews on these sites belong to the listing, and the listing exists whether or not you claim it. What you need is the login, so you can reply to reviews and fix wrong details.

  • Yelp: log in at biz.yelp.com. Under your account settings, check which users have access. Ad contracts are separate from the listing; read the cancel terms before you sign one.
  • BBB: your business can have a BBB profile even if you never joined. Find out which email is on file if you've claimed it.
  • Angi, Thumbtack, Nextdoor, HomeAdvisor: make sure the login email is yours, not a past employee's or agency's.

Do this today: make one list of every site you've ever signed up for, with the login email beside each. When an employee or agency leaves, you'll know exactly what to change.

11

Photos and written content

In the US, the person who creates a photo or an article usually owns the copyright, unless a written agreement says otherwise. So the photos a hired photographer took of your crew, or the blog posts an agency wrote, might not be yours to reuse after you leave.

  • Photos you or your crew took on your own phones are yours. Keep the originals in one folder you control.
  • Look for a line in your contract that says content made for you belongs to you, or is "assigned" to you. "Licensed to you during the term" means you lose it when you leave.
  • Stock photos are licensed, often to the agency, not to you. Ask before you reuse them on a new site.

This isn't legal advice. If you have a big library of paid content at stake, have a lawyer read the contract.

12

Before you sign, and before you leave

Before you sign: ask for wording like this

Ownership clause to ask for

"All accounts, domains, phone numbers, website content, photos and data created or used for my business are owned by my business and set up in its name. Provider has user or manager access only. When the agreement ends, Provider will hand over all access, files and numbers within 10 business days, at no extra charge."

A good company will sign something close to this without a fight. Also ask these out loud and write down the answers:

  • "Whose name will the domain and phone numbers be in?"
  • "If I leave, what do I walk away with, and is there a fee for it?"
  • "Will I be the owner on Google, Facebook and the ads account, with you as a user?"
  • "How much notice do I need to give, and is there a cancellation fee?"

Before you leave: do it in this order

  1. Don't give notice yet.Go through this guide first and write down what's yours, what isn't, and what you need from them.
  2. Get yourself added as owner or admin everywherewhile you're still on good terms. Most of it is a five-minute favor.
  3. Download your history:ads results, call logs from any tracking numbers, website files, photos and the page list.
  4. Start any phone number portsand wait for them to finish.
  5. Give notice in writingwith a numbered list of everything you need handed over, and a date.
  6. Once you hold everything,remove their access, change passwords, and check the recovery phone and email on each account.
Same goes for employees

When an office manager or family member who set things up leaves, run this checklist the same week. Old logins are how profiles get changed or deleted months later.

Quick answers

My marketing company says they'll give me everything if I leave. Is that enough?

Get it in writing, and better yet, get yourself added as owner or admin on every account now, while you're on good terms. A promise made at the start is worth less on the day you cancel.

Can my agency keep my domain if I leave?

If the domain is registered in their name or sits in their registrar account, they control it in practice. Ask them to move it into a registrar account in your name. If you are listed as the registrant, the registrar must let you transfer it out under ICANN's transfer rules.

Can I keep my phone number if I leave?

You can port a number if you are the customer of record on the account that holds it. If the number sits in your agency's account, you need them to transfer or port it to you, so get that promise in the contract before you put the number on your truck.

Who owns the photos my marketing company took?

In the US, the person who creates a photo or article usually owns the copyright unless there's a written agreement that transfers it. Make sure your contract says the content made for you is yours, and that you get the original files when you leave.

Will I lose my Google reviews if I change marketing companies?

No, not if you keep the same Google Business Profile. Reviews belong to the profile. That's why you should be its Primary Owner, so changing companies never means starting a new profile.
