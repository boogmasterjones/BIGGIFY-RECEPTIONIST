01

The first screen

Most of your visitors are on a phone, often standing next to a leak, a dead AC unit or a tripped breaker. What they see before they scroll decides whether they call you or hit Back. That first screen has one job: get the call.

It must answer four questions in five seconds

  1. What do you do?In the words customers use. "Water heater repair and replacement", not "Complete home comfort solutions".
  2. Where?Your main town or county, in the headline or right under it. Someone two states away should know at a glance that you're not for them.
  3. How do I reach you?A big Call button with the number written out, and a second button that jumps to your quote form.
  4. Why you?One or two true facts: licensed, a strong rating if you really have it, how fast you call back. And a real photo of you or your work.

Headline formulas you can copy

FormulaExample
[Service] in [area]Drain cleaning and sewer repair across Greater Tulsa
[The problem], fixed in [area]No AC? Repairs and new systems in Mesa and Gilbert
[Trade] for [town] homes, [true detail]Licensed electricians for Boise homes, from panels to EV chargers

Keep it to three lines on a phone, one idea. If you must choose, clear beats clever. Under it, one plain sentence in your own voice: "We repair and replace water heaters across Mesa, Gilbert and Chandler, and we call back the same day." Only say what's true.

The phone number: big, written out, tappable

  • Write the number as text, never inside a picture. People copy it, and Google reads it.
  • Make it tap-to-call. On any platform, the link looks like this (+1, then 10 digits, no spaces):
<a href="tel:+19415550123">Call (941) 555-0123</a>
  • In the header of every page. On phones, add a Call bar that stays at the bottom of the screen once they scroll past the top.
  • Thumb-sized buttons: at least 44 pixels tall. Call comes first, then "Get a quote".
  • One number everywhere on the site. More in chapter 12.

Where the form goes

Not squeezed into the top of a phone screen, where it pushes your headline and Call button down. This is the order we use on the sites we build:

  1. First screen: headline, Call button, "Get a quote" button, a real photo.
  2. Real job photos, so they see the work is real.
  3. The quote form. The "Get a quote" button jumps straight here.
  4. Reviews, word for word.
  5. Everything else: services, towns, about you, questions.

On service and town pages, put the form high too, not only at the very bottom.

✓ Do

  • A real photo of you, your truck or a finished job
  • The number written out and tappable
  • One clear headline with your town in it
  • A still photo that loads fast

✕ Don't

  • Rotating sliders (nobody waits for slide 3)
  • Pop-ups or chat bubbles covering the Call button
  • "Welcome to our website"
  • Stock photos of handshakes or models in hard hats
The 5-second test

Open your site on your phone. Hand it to someone who doesn't know your business and take it back after 5 seconds. Ask: what do we do, where, and how would you reach us? If they miss one, fix the first screen before anything else in this playbook.

  • First-screen check
  • Says what we do, in customer words
  • Says where we work
  • Call button with the number written out, and it dials when tapped
  • "Get a quote" button jumps to the form
  • A real photo, not stock
  • No slider, pop-up or chat bubble covering anything
02

The pages you need

Google ranks pages, not websites. A one-page site can only really rank for one thing. Each service you want calls for, and each town you want calls from, needs its own page.

PageAims forMust have
Home[Trade] [main town], e.g. "plumber Tulsa"The first screen from chapter 1, photos, form, reviews, links to every service and town page
One page per service[Service] [area], e.g. "water heater repair Mesa"Only services you really do and are licensed for. Real depth (chapter 3)
One page per town[Trade] [that town]Up to about 7 towns you really serve, each written fresh (chapter 4)
AboutTrustYou, your story, your photo, your license and insurance if you have them
ContactThe callPhone, form, the towns you serve. A home-based business doesn't need to show its street address
Privacy, thank-youThe basicsA privacy page (your form collects details). A thank-you page after the form (chapter 11)

Picking your towns

  • Start with your home town. Then the biggest, best-known towns within about 50 miles that you really drive to.
  • About 7 is plenty to start. Seven good pages beat 40 thin ones.
  • Don't pad the list with tiny places just to have more pages.
Never a page for work you don't do

A "gas line repair" page when your license doesn't cover gas brings the wrong calls and can get you in trouble with your licensing board. Every page should be a job you'd happily take tomorrow.

Your homepage title should be different from your main service page and your home-town page, so the three don't compete with each other for the same search.

03

Service pages done right

A service page that says "We offer quality AC repair. Call today!" in three lines won't rank and won't convince anyone. Thin pages don't rank. A good service page answers what the customer is wondering before they call.

What goes on every service page

  1. The problem, in their words.What it looks, sounds or smells like. "Water pooling under the tank", "breaker keeps tripping".
  2. How you fix it.Your actual process: what you check first, repair vs. replace, how long it usually takes.
  3. The options.Brands, materials or types you really install. These are also words people search for.
  4. How pricing works.A range if you're comfortable, or how you quote ("we price the job upfront before we start"). Only if it's true.
  5. Real photos of this job.Not your general gallery, the photos for this service.
  6. The form, high on the page,plus the Call button.
  7. 3 to 6 real questionscustomers ask you on the phone, with short answers.
  8. A closing sectionwith a short list of the variations you handle and the towns you serve, each town linking to its page.

Length: around 1,000 words tends to work for a main service page. That's a target for depth, not a quota. Every paragraph should answer something real. Stuffing the same phrase over and over can get a page ignored.

Use the words people actually type

Work these in naturally, where they read like normal English:

  • "[service] near me" and "[service] [town] [state]"
  • "[town] [trade]", e.g. "Boise electrician"
  • The variations: "tankless water heater", "heat pump", "porcelain tile", "panel upgrade"
  • True trust words: "licensed", "insured", "free estimate" (only if each is true)
Do this today

Write down the 5 questions you answer most on the phone. Each one is a paragraph (or a FAQ) on a service page. You already know the answers better than any copywriter.

04

Town pages, not copies

This is the rule that gets skipped most, and it matters most. Many sites have 20 town pages that are the same text with the town name swapped. Google's spam policies call these "doorway" pages, and they tend to get ignored or pull the whole site down.

What makes a town page real

  • Its own opening paragraph, written fresh. Not a template with the name swapped.
  • Local detail that's true: neighborhoods you work in, landmarks people know, what the homes are like there (age, slab or basement, older wiring, septic or sewer).
  • Local conditions that affect your trade: hard water, salt air near the coast, clay soil that shifts, freezing winters, older homes built in a building boom.
  • The town's size as a real detail ("a town of about 60,000").
  • Real jobs and photos from that town, if you have them.
  • Links to your homepage and to 1 or 2 nearby town pages, with short, varied link words. Not a block of 30 links in the footer.
  • The form and Call button, high on the page.

Length: around 800 words tends to work, every one of them about that town.

✓ Reads like you've been there

  • "A lot of homes on the east side were built in the 1970s, and many still have their original water lines. That's most of our repipes here."
  • "From our shop it's about 20 minutes, so same-day visits are usually possible."

✕ Reads like a template

  • "We are proud to serve the wonderful community of [Town] with quality plumbing services."
  • The same three paragraphs as every other town page
The swap test

Open two of your town pages side by side. Swap the town names in your head. If both still read as true, rewrite them. Re-read them every time you add a new town page.

Town pages help your website rank in nearby towns. Your Google Business Profile works differently: adding service areas there doesn't make you rank in those towns. More on that in our guide Change your Google profile without getting hidden.

05

Titles and meta descriptions

The title is the blue headline in Google's results. The meta description is often the gray text under it. They're the first thing a searcher reads, and the title is one of the clearest signals of what your page is about.

The title formula

[Service] in [Town], [ST] | [Business name]
  • Aim for about 60 characters. Google cuts longer titles off by width. If yours runs long, drop the business name before you drop the town.
  • Every page gets its own title. Never the same title on two pages.
  • No "Home", no "Welcome", no ALL CAPS, no "Best" or "#1".
PageTitle
HomePlumber in Tulsa, OK, Repairs to Repipes | [Business]
ServiceWater Heater Repair in Tulsa, OK | [Business]
TownBroken Arrow Plumber, Older Homes & New Builds | [Business]
ContactContact [Business], Plumbing in Tulsa County

The meta description formula

[What you do] in [towns]. [One true trust fact]. [What to do next].

Aim for 120 to 160 characters. Example (152 characters):

"Water heater repair and replacement in Tulsa and Broken Arrow. Licensed and insured, upfront quotes before we start. Call now or request a quote online."

Google may rewrite them

Google sometimes shows different text than what you wrote, especially when yours doesn't match the page. A clear, honest title and description that match the page get used more often.

Where to change them

  • WordPress: the SEO box under each page (from your SEO plugin).
  • Wix and Squarespace: each page's SEO settings.
  • Custom site: the <title> tag and <meta name="description"> in each page's code. Ask your designer.

Do this today: search Google for site:yourdomain.com. You'll see every page Google has, with its title. Look for duplicates, "Home", and titles with no town in them.

06

Headings

Headings are the big bold lines that break up a page. Google uses them to understand the page, and phone readers use them to skim. Most sites get them wrong in the same few ways.

  • One main heading (the H1) per page, saying what the page is about in natural words: "Water heater repair in Tulsa".
  • The words in your H1 should also appear in the text below. A heading whose words never show up again is a weak signal.
  • Sections use H2, sub-sections H3. Don't skip levels or pick a heading size for looks.
  • Headings in proportion to the text. A short page with 15 headings looks stuffed.
  • Never make the phone number or "Call now" a heading. Those are buttons.

A service page outline you can copy

H1  Water heater repair in Tulsa
  H2  Signs your water heater needs repair
  H2  Repair or replace? How we decide with you
  H2  Tank, tankless and heat pump water heaters
  H2  What a water heater repair costs
  H2  Questions we get asked
  H2  Water heater service across Tulsa County
    H3  Water heater work we do
    H3  Towns we serve

Most platforms let you pick "Heading 1, 2, 3" from a menu. If yours shows "Title, Subtitle, Heading", ask your designer which is the H1, and check that there's only one per page.

07

Real photos and alt text

Customers can tell a stock photo in half a second, and it quietly tells them you're hiding something. AI-made "job photos" are worse: they show work you didn't do. Your own photos, even from a phone, beat both.

W
How we do itWyatt, Biggify

We build our clients' sites custom, with real job photos only. If a client doesn't have a photo for something yet, we leave the spot empty until they do rather than fill it with stock.

What to shoot (start a folder today)

  • Before and after, from the same spot and angle.
  • You and your crew at the truck or on the job. People hire people.
  • Finished work: one wide shot, one close-up of the detail.
  • In progress: shows skill and care.
  • Your truck or van with the lettering, if it's clean and the info on it is current.

Phone camera rules

  • Wipe the lens. Shoot in daylight or with the lights on. Hold the phone sideways for wide shots.
  • Ask the homeowner before you shoot. Keep house numbers, faces of family members and anything personal out of frame.
  • Check the background: no mess, no competitor's truck, nothing that shows an old phone number.
  • Full color. No black-and-white filters.

Before you upload

  1. Shrink it. Phone photos are huge. About 1,600 pixels wide is plenty for the biggest spot on a site. Smaller spots need smaller files.
  2. Compress it to WebP or a compressed JPG. Aim for most photos under about 200 KB.
  3. Remove the location data. Phone photos can carry the GPS spot where they were taken, which can be a customer's home. Many resize tools and platforms strip it. Check yours.
  4. Name the file for what it shows: tankless-water-heater-install-broken-arrow.webp, not IMG_4471.jpg.

Alt text: what the photo shows, in plain words

Alt text is the short description screen readers read aloud and Google reads to understand the image. Formula: what's in the photo + where, when it's natural.

WeakGood
image1New tankless water heater mounted on a garage wall in Broken Arrow
plumber tulsa best plumber tulsa okOur plumber replacing a corroded shut-off valve under a kitchen sink
roofFinished architectural shingle roof on a two-story home in Edmond

Only name a brand or material if it's really what's in the photo.

08

Reviews on your site

Your Google reviews already convince people. Put the best ones on your site, right below the quote form, where people decide.

How to show them

  • Word for word. Don't fix spelling or trim out the awkward bit. Real reviews read real.
  • Credit them: first name and last initial, "Google review", and the month it was posted.
  • Pick ones that mention a service or town: "fixed our water heater in Broken Arrow the same day" sells more than "great job".
  • Show your rating only if it's true today, e.g. "5.0 on Google", and check it every month. Ratings move.
  • Link to your Google reviews so people can read the rest.

Pasted text or a live widget?

OptionGoodWatch out for
Pasted text (our pick)Loads instantly, Google can read it, you choose the best onesAdd new ones every month or two
Live review widgetUpdates on its ownExtra script that can slow your site; some show only stars, not words; often a monthly fee
Never fake or "improve" a review

The FTC has a rule against fake reviews and testimonials, and it comes with real fines. Don't write reviews, don't edit what a customer said, and don't put your own reviews into star-rating code to try to get stars in Google. Google doesn't show those stars for a business's own reviews anyway.

09

Trust items that are true

Trust items are the little facts that make a stranger comfortable letting you into their home. They only work if they're true, and some of them the law requires.

Your license number

  • Some states require your license number on all your advertising, and treat your website as advertising. Florida and California are two well-known examples. Check your state's licensing board for the exact rule in your trade.
  • Show it the way the board does: the label and the number, e.g. "Lic. #XX0000000". Put it in the footer of every page and on the About and Contact pages.
  • Look yourself up on the board's website first and make sure the number, the name and the status match. A typo in a license number looks worse than none.
  • Working under another company's license, or as a DBA? Say exactly that, the way the board lists it.

Everything else

Trust itemShow it ifWhere
"Licensed"You hold an active license for that workFirst screen, footer, About
"Insured"Your policy is active, and you'd show the certificate if askedFirst screen or About
Years in businessOne true number, the same everywhereAbout, first screen
"Free estimates"You never charge to quoteButtons, service pages
WarrantyIt's in writing and you can say what it coversService pages
Brand certificationsThey're current; check the maker's dealer lookupService pages
HoursSomeone actually answers in those hours. "24/7" only if a person or service picks up at 2 amHeader or footer, Contact

✓ Builds trust

  • Your name and face on the About page
  • A license number people can look up
  • Real reviews with first names
  • "We give you a written quote before we start" (if you do)

✕ Costs trust

  • "#1", "Best in town", "Guaranteed lowest price"
  • Badges you didn't earn, or "As seen on" logos
  • Counters like "10,000 happy customers" you can't back up
  • Hours nobody answers

Do this today: list every claim on your site (licensed, insured, years, warranty, hours). Next to each, write how you'd prove it. If you can't, fix it or take it off.

10

Speed and phones

A slow site loses the impatient caller, and someone with a flooded kitchen is impatient. Google also uses page experience in ranking. The good news: most slow trade sites are slow for the same three or four reasons.

Test it (5 minutes)

  1. Go to pagespeed.web.dev, enter your homepage, and look at the Mobile tab first.
  2. Test a service page and a town page too, not only the homepage.
  3. Then open your site on your own phone on cell data, with Wi-Fi off. That's how most customers see it.
Google's measureWhat it means"Good" is
LCPHow fast the main thing (usually the top photo or headline) shows up2.5 seconds or less
INPHow fast the page reacts when someone taps200 milliseconds or less
CLSHow much things jump around while loading (the "I tapped the wrong button" problem)0.1 or less

The fixes that usually matter most

  1. Shrink your photos.The number one cause. See chapter 7. A single uncompressed phone photo can weigh more than the rest of the page.
  2. Cut extra add-ons.Chat widgets, sliders, pop-ups, review widgets, several tracking tags. Each one adds weight. Keep only what earns its place.
  3. Load the top photo right away, the rest as people scroll.Below-the-screen photos should "lazy load". The top photo shouldn't.
  4. Give photos a set sizeso the page doesn't jump while they load.
  5. On WordPress:fewer plugins, a caching plugin, and a lightweight theme. Page builders loaded with effects are a common cause of slow sites.

Phone checks

  • On your own phone
  • No sideways scrolling on any page
  • Text is readable without zooming
  • Buttons are easy to hit with a thumb (about 44 pixels or more), and not crammed together
  • Nothing covers the Call button: no pop-up, no cookie bar, no chat bubble
  • Menu opens, closes, and every link works
  • Forms are easy to fill in, and the keyboard shows numbers for the phone box
11

Forms that actually arrive

A broken form is the worst kind of broken: the site looks fine, customers fill it in, and the leads go nowhere. Nobody tells you. You just get quieter.

W
It happened to usWyatt, Biggify

Two client quote forms once broke without anyone noticing, and leads were lost before we caught it. Now forms get tested regularly. That's why this chapter exists.

What a good quote form asks

  • Name
  • Phone or email: one is enough. Say so: "Phone or email, we only need one way to reach you."
  • What do you need? A short box or tap-to-pick choices.
  • Town or ZIP, so you know if it's in your area.
  • Optional extras only: how soon, a photo upload, details.

Every box needs a real label you can see, not just gray hint text that disappears when they start typing.

Where the leads go

  • To an inbox someone checks every day, and ideally to two people or to your phone as a text.
  • After sending, the visitor lands on a thank-you page that says what happens next ("We'll call you within a couple of hours") and shows the Call button.
  • Stop spam without puzzles. A hidden "spam trap" box that only bots fill in, or an invisible spam check, works better than making real customers pick out traffic lights.
  • Will you text people back? Add an optional, unticked box that asks permission to text, with plain words about what you'll send and "Reply STOP to opt out". Phone carriers expect a clear opt-in like this before a business texts.

Why forms break (with no warning)

  • A plugin, theme or platform update
  • A redesign, or moving the site to a new host
  • Your email address or email provider changed
  • The form service's free plan hit its monthly limit
  • Lead emails started landing in spam, or the mailbox is full
  • Someone changed who gets the notifications

The 3-minute form test (put it on your calendar)

  1. On your phone, fill in your own formas a customer would. Use your name with "TEST" in front.
  2. Check you landed on the thank-you page.
  3. Check it arrivedwherever leads should go: the inbox, the text, your CRM. Check the spam folder too.
  4. Do it on every different form(homepage, contact page, any booking form).
  5. Test once a week, and the same day after any changeto the site, the plugins or your email.
12

Call tracking basics

For most trades, the phone is where the money is. Without tracking, you can't tell whether the website is bringing in calls or just sitting there. There are two levels.

Level 1: count the taps (free)

Your analytics can record every time someone taps a Call button on your site. That tells you how many people tried to call from the website, and from which button. It doesn't tell you if the call connected.

With Google Analytics 4 installed, a tap can be recorded like this (your designer can also set it up without touching each link):

<a href="tel:+19415550123"
   onclick="gtag('event','call_click',{'placement':'header'})">
  Call (941) 555-0123
</a>

Use a different placement label for each spot (header, first screen, footer, town page) so you can see which buttons get used.

Level 2: a tracking number (paid)

A call-tracking service gives you a number that forwards to your phone and logs every call: when, how long, and where the caller came from. Some can swap in a different number for visitors who came from an ad ("dynamic number insertion").

RuleWhy
The number is in your name, or the contract says it moves to youIf the company keeps it when you leave, your old calls ring someone else
One main number across your siteCustomers and Google see one business, not three
On your Google profile: tracking number as main, your real number as additionalKeeps your listing matching everything else
Keep the number in one place on the siteChanging it later is one edit, not a hunt through 30 pages
Recording calls? Announce itSome states require everyone on the call to agree to recording

For the full rules on changing phone numbers on Google without trouble, see Change your Google profile without getting hidden. And if the website brings calls you can't pick up, run your numbers through the free missed-call calculator.

13

Analytics and Search Console

Two free Google tools tell you what's working. Google Analytics shows what people do on your site. Search Console shows what they searched to find you. Set both up in your own Google account, then give your designer access.

Google Analytics 4 (about 15 minutes)

  1. Go to analytics.google.comand sign in with the Google account you use for your business.
  2. Create a propertynamed clearly, e.g. "[Business] website". Pick your time zone and currency.
  3. Add a Web data streamwith your site's address. Google gives you a Measurement ID that starts with "G-".
  4. Install it.WordPress, Wix and Squarespace each have a place to paste the G- ID. On a custom site, the tag goes in the head of every page.
  5. Check it works:open your site on your phone, then look at Reports → Realtime. You should see yourself.
  6. Mark what matters as key events:form sent and Call button taps. Those are your leads.

Analytics only counts visits from the day it's installed, so set it up before launch, not "later".

Google Search Console (about 15 minutes)

  1. Go to search.google.com/search-consoleand add a Domain property (just yourdomain.com).
  2. Verify itby adding the text record Google gives you at the company where you bought your domain (the DNS settings). Most registrars have a help page for "add TXT record".
  3. Submit your sitemap.Usually yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml. Some WordPress SEO plugins use sitemap_index.xml. Open it in your browser first to see which.
  4. Check Pagesfor anything "not indexed" that should be.

What to look at (once a month)

  • Search Console → Performance: the searches you show up for. Sort by impressions. Searches with lots of impressions but few clicks are your next title fix or page improvement.
  • Analytics: leads (form sends + call taps) per month. That's the number that matters, not visitors.
Whose account is it in?

If your designer set these up in their own Google account, ask them to add you as an Administrator (Analytics) and Owner (Search Console). If you part ways, your history should stay with you.

Local schema

Schema is a small block of code, invisible to visitors, that tells Google in plain facts who you are: your name, trade, phone, hours and the area you serve. It doesn't get you to the top by itself, but it removes the guesswork.

Pick your most specific business type

Examples that exist on schema.org: Plumber, Electrician, HVACBusiness, RoofingContractor, GeneralContractor, HousePainter, Locksmith. If none fits, use HomeAndConstructionBusiness, or LocalBusiness.

A template to fill in

Replace everything in [brackets]. A home-based business can leave out the street address and list its service area instead.

<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Plumber",
  "name": "[Business name, exactly as on Google]",
  "url": "https://[yourdomain.com]/",
  "telephone": "+1[10 digits]",
  "image": "https://[yourdomain.com]/[a real job photo].jpg",
  "areaServed": [
    {"@type": "City", "name": "[Town], [ST]"},
    {"@type": "City", "name": "[Town], [ST]"}
  ],
  "openingHours": "Mo-Fr 07:00-18:00",
  "sameAs": [
    "[link to your Google Business Profile]",
    "[link to your Facebook page]"
  ]
}
</script>
  • Name, phone and hours must match your site and your Google profile exactly.
  • One business block per page, not two plugins each adding their own. Check by testing.
  • No star-rating code for your own reviews. Google doesn't show stars for a business's own reviews of itself, and fake ratings break the rules.
  • On WordPress, many SEO plugins can add this for you from a settings screen. Fill it in fully.

Test it: paste your page address into Google's Rich Results Test (search.google.com/test/rich-results) and the Schema Markup Validator (validator.schema.org). Fix anything marked as an error.

15

A blog: when it's worth it

A blog is not step one. Plenty of trade sites have 50 posts and weak service pages. Do it in this order: first screen, service pages, town pages, forms. Then a blog can help.

✓ Worth writing

  • A question Search Console shows people search, that no page of yours answers
  • Cost questions: "How much does a panel upgrade cost in [area]?"
  • "Repair or replace?" posts, from your real experience
  • Local rules: permits in your county, local code changes
  • "What's that noise / smell / leak?" posts with your own photos

✕ Not worth it

  • "10 tips for a happy home" generic filler
  • Posts churned out by AI that nobody checked
  • A post that competes with your own service page for the same search
  • Posting because "Google likes fresh content"

Rules for a post that earns its keep

  • Answer one question fully, in roughly 800 to 1,200 words.
  • Use your real experience and your own photos. That's the one thing a competitor can't copy.
  • Link to the matching service page, with the Call button and form on the post.
  • One or two good posts a month is plenty. Ten thin ones is worse than none.
  • If a page of yours already shows up for the topic, improve that page instead of writing a new post that competes with it.

The monthly 15-minute checklist

Websites break quietly. Fifteen minutes on the same day every month catches most problems before they cost you calls. Put it on your calendar now.

  • Every month
  • Fill in every form on your phone as a test; confirm it arrived (3 min)
  • Tap the Call button on the homepage and one other page; it should ring (1 min)
  • Open the homepage on cell data; anything slow, broken or covered? (1 min)
  • Rating and reviews on the site still true; add one or two new reviews (3 min)
  • Hours, phone, services and license still correct everywhere (2 min)
  • Search Console: any errors, and the top searches with lots of impressions but few clicks (3 min)
  • Analytics: leads this month (form sends + call taps) vs. last month (1 min)
  • Add a few new job photos to the right service or town page (1 min)
  • Every few months
  • Domain auto-renew is on and the card on file hasn't expired
  • Run PageSpeed again; add-ons creep in
  • Re-read your town pages side by side (the swap test)
  • Check who has admin access to the site and your Google accounts; remove old staff and old agencies

Quick answers

Does it matter if my site is WordPress, Wix, Squarespace or custom?
Less than people think. Every rule in this playbook works on all of them. What matters is the first screen, real pages for each service and town, speed, forms that arrive, and you owning it.
How many town pages should I have?
Start with about 7: your home town plus the biggest towns within roughly 50 miles that you really serve. Each has to be written fresh about that town. Seven good ones beat 40 copies.
Can I use stock photos until I have my own?
We don't. Customers spot stock instantly, and AI-made job photos show work you didn't do. Leave the spot out and start taking photos on your next job.
Will schema or a blog get me to the top of Google?
Not on their own. They help Google understand you. Service pages, town pages, reviews and your Google Business Profile do more of the heavy lifting for local trades.
How often should I test my forms?
Weekly, and the same day after any change to the site, its plugins or your email. It takes 3 minutes.
Someone promised me first-page rankings. Is that real?
No one can promise that. Google doesn't sell rankings. Treat "guaranteed rankings" as a reason to walk away.
