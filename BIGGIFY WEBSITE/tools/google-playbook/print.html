The Complete Google Profile, Reviews & Photos Playbook

Set your Google profile up right, then work it every week.

When someone near you searches "plumber near me" or "roof leak repair", Google shows a map with three businesses under it. Most local calls start there. Google says it picks those three on relevance (do you match the search), distance (how close you are) and prominence (how well known you are, reviews included). It also says nobody can pay for a better spot on that map.

You can't move your shop closer to every customer. But relevance and prominence are mostly in your hands: the right category, a complete profile, real photos, steady reviews and replies. This playbook covers all of it, setting by setting, plus the routine that keeps it working. Every chapter ends with something to do today.

Part 1 · Set it up right

01

Before you touch anything

Two things first: make sure the profile is really yours, and learn the one rule that keeps it safe while you work on it.

Own it on an email you control

RoleWhoWhat they can do
Primary OwnerYou, on an email you control. A business email is best, so it stays with the business.Everything, including adding and removing people.
ManagerYour marketing company, office manager, web person.The day-to-day work. They can't remove you.

Check it now: open your profile while logged in, then the menu → Business Profile settings → People and access. You should be the Primary Owner. Remove anyone you don't know or no longer work with.

How we set it up

Our clients are the Primary Owner of their profiles; we're added as a Manager. We can do the work, and the client can remove us any day they want.

If someone else holds the profile (an old agency, an ex-employee), get it back before you do anything else. Our guide Who owns your stuff? walks through it.

The one-change-a-week rule

A few fields are the "core" of your business in Google's eyes: name, address or service area, main phone, main category and website link. Change one and Google may re-check you. Change several in one afternoon and it can look like a hijack, which is how profiles get hidden or suspended.

The rule

One core change per week. Photos, posts, services, the description and attributes are safe any time. Chapter 19 has the full safe-change rules.

  • Do today
  • I'm the Primary Owner, on an email I control.
  • I removed anyone in People and access I don't work with.
  • I know which fields are "core" and won't change two in one week.
02

Setup and verification

An unverified profile doesn't show properly, and your posts and photos can sit "pending". If your dashboard says to verify, finish that before anything else.

Getting set up

  1. Search for your business first at business.google.com. A customer or Google may already have made a profile. Claim that one instead of making a second.
  2. Use your real-world name, exactly as on your truck, invoices and license. No town or service stuffed in.
  3. Pick your main category (chapter 3) and say whether customers come to you, you go to them, or both (chapter 7).
  4. Add your main phone and website. The website should be your own site, not a Facebook page or a redirect link.

Verification

Google chooses which way you verify. You may be offered a video recording, a phone call or text, an email, a live video call or a postcard. For a trade business it's often a video. If you get a video, plan it before you hit record:

  • Show where you are: the street sign or house number, then your truck or shop.
  • Show that it's a real business: lettered vehicle, tools and equipment, signage.
  • Show that you run it: unlock the shop or the truck, or show a business document like your license or registration.
  • Record it in one continuous take. No editing.
While verification is pending

Don't change the name, address or category, and don't request a second code or postcard. A new code cancels the first one. Wait it out.

  • Do today
  • My profile says "Verified" (no banner asking me to verify).
  • There's only one profile for my business. If I find a duplicate, I ask Google support to merge or remove it.
03

Picking your main category

Your main (primary) category tells Google what you are. It's the biggest relevance setting you control, and the one we find wrong most often.

What Google's rules say

  • Be specific. Categories should be "as specific as possible, but representative of your main business."
  • Use as few as you need. Choose the fewest categories it takes to describe your core business.
  • Only Google's list counts. If it isn't offered when you start typing, it isn't a category.

Pick it in 5 steps (15 minutes)

  1. Write down the job you want most. The work that brings the most money and the most calls. For most owners it's the trade itself.
  2. Search like a customer. On your phone, logged out (a private window): "[your trade] near me" and "[your main service] [your town]". Look at the map, not the ads.
  3. Read the top 3's categories. On Google Maps the main category shows under each business's name and stars. Write all three down.
  4. Choose the most specific one that's your whole business. If all three use "Plumber" and you're a plumber, use "Plumber". If you only do water heaters, a water heater category may fit better than the broad trade.
  5. Change it once, then leave it alone. Give it 2 to 6 weeks before you judge. No other core change that week.

04

Extra categories

Extra (secondary) categories tell Google about the other real work you do. Google lets you add several, but its rule is the fewest you need. For most trade businesses that's a handful, not ten.

How to choose them

  1. List your services that bring real calls. Not one-off jobs. The ones you'd happily do every week.
  2. Type each into the category box. If Google offers a matching category, it's a candidate. If not, it belongs in the Services list (chapter 5).
  3. Check what the top 3 use. Google only shows the main category in public. Several free browser extensions made for local search can show the extra ones too. Never install one that asks you to sign in with your Google account.
  4. Add only what's true. If you don't install generators, don't add a generator category because a competitor has it.

05

Services and products

Google says complete, accurate profiles are more likely to show up. The Services list is where you spell out every job, in the words customers use.

Services: how to fill them in

  1. Open your profile → Edit profile → Services (on some screens it's under Edit services).
  2. Tick every suggested service you really do. Google suggests some for each category.
  3. Add your own for the rest. Use the customer's words: "AC repair", not "climate solutions". "Water heater replacement", not "hydronic solutions".
  4. Add a one- or two-sentence description to your most important services: what's included and who it's for. No keyword lists.
  5. Prices only if they're real and you'll honor them. A "from" price you never charge causes angry calls.

06

Your description

You get 750 characters. On most screens only the first part shows before "More", so lead with what you do and where.

Google's rules for it

  • No links of any kind.
  • No focus on promotions, prices or sales. Those go in posts.
  • No irrelevant or low-quality content. A list of towns and keywords counts as that.

Fill-in-the-blanks template

Copy and fill in

[Business] is a [trade] serving [main town] and [2–4 nearby towns/county]. We handle [top 3 services in plain words] for homeowners [and businesses]. [One true thing that sets you apart: family-owned since ___, licensed and insured, same-day service when we can, upfront written quotes]. [Who answers: "You'll talk to [Owner name] or our office, not a call center."] Call or book online and we'll [what happens next: "give you a straight answer on what it'll take"].

Only true things

Every claim in your description should be something you can back up: license, years in business, what you really offer. If it's true, say it plainly. If it isn't, leave it out.

  • Do today
  • The first sentence says what we do and where.
  • No links, no prices, no list of towns.
  • Everything in it is true and we can prove it.
07

Address vs service area

This one surprises owners the most. Google ranks you mostly from where your business is, not from the towns you type in.

Your setupWhat to do
Customers never come to you (you drive to them), and you work from homeUse your real address and hide it. Then add service areas.
You have a shop or showroom with staff during the hours you list, and customers visitShow the address. You can add service areas too.
You rent a mailbox, a UPS Store box or a virtual officeNot eligible. Google's rules don't accept PO boxes, remote mailboxes or virtual offices. They get profiles removed.

Service areas

  • You can add up to 20 service areas: cities, ZIP codes or other areas.
  • The whole area should stay within about 2 hours' drive of where you're based.
  • Service areas tell customers where you'll go. They don't make you rank in those towns. Adding 30 towns doesn't make you show up in 30 towns.
  • To win a town farther out: give it a page on your website with work you've really done there, and earn reviews from customers in that town.
Check the pin

Open your profile on Google Maps and look where the pin sits. If it's off (wrong street, middle of a field), fix the address. Google measures your distance from that pin.

  • Do today
  • Address shown or hidden correctly for how we work.
  • Service areas are real places we go, within about 2 hours.
  • No mailbox or virtual office address anywhere.
08

Hours, and the honest 24/7 rule

Google asks for your regular customer-facing hours. And being open when someone searches matters: a business showing "Closed" at 7 pm gets shown far less at 7 pm. That's why longer real hours win calls.

The honest 24/7 rule

List "Open 24 hours" only if a call at 2 am gets picked up and the job gets taken. That can be you, an on-call tech, an answering service or an AI receptionist. If the 2 am caller gets voicemail, it isn't 24/7.

✓ True 24/7

  • Someone answers every call, day and night
  • They take the details and you call back or dispatch
  • Your website and Facebook say the same hours

✕ Fake 24/7

  • "24/7" with voicemail after 6 pm
  • It burns the emergency caller who needed you most
  • A known trigger for edits and suspensions

Other hours to set

  • Holiday hours: set "special hours" a week ahead for every holiday. If you're open, say so. That's when competitors are closed.
  • More hours: some categories let you add separate hours for things like emergency service or phone lines. Use them only if they're true.
  • By appointment only? Google's rules say some appointment-only businesses shouldn't list hours. Most trades take calls during set hours, so list the hours you answer the phone.
  • Do today
  • Our hours are the hours someone really answers.
  • If we show 24/7, I tested a late-night call and someone picked up.
  • Holiday hours are set for the next holiday.
09

Attributes

Attributes are the small tick-boxes under Edit profile → More (or Business information). They show as little badges and details that help a customer choose. What you see depends on your category.

  • Service options: onsite services, online estimates, online appointments.
  • Owner identity: veteran-owned, women-owned and similar, if true. Some customers search for these.
  • Payments: cards, mobile payments, checks. Saves a "do you take cards?" call.
  • Accessibility: if customers visit, wheelchair-accessible entrance or parking, if true.
  • Languages: if someone on your team speaks another language with customers.

Customers can suggest attributes too, so check them every few months and fix anything wrong.

  • Do today
  • I ticked every attribute that's true for us.
  • Nothing ticked that isn't true.

Photos

Photos are where customers decide if you look like the real deal. Real job photos beat stock photos every time, because stock photos look like everyone else.

The shot list

ShotWhy it helps
Your lettered truck or vanProves you're a real, local business. Also great for verification.
Before and after, same angleThe most convincing photo there is. Stand in the same spot both times.
The finished job, wide shotThe new floor, the new roof, the clean driveway.
A detail close-upClean cuts, neat wiring, straight grout lines. Shows care.
The problem you fixedThe rusted water heater, the cracked tile, the burned outlet. Customers recognize their own problem.
You and your team at workPeople hire people. Get permission from everyone in the shot.
Owner photoA friendly, clear photo of you. The face behind the phone number.
Logo and cover photoLogo square and clean. Cover: your best real job or your truck.
Shop or office outside (only if customers visit)Helps them find you.

How often

A few new photos every week, from that week's jobs. Steady beats 80 photos in one afternoon. Make it a habit: before you pack up, take the "after" shot.

The rules

  • Real only. Google asks for photos that represent reality, with no significant alterations or excessive filters or AI. Crop and brighten, yes. Fake, no.
  • No AI images, no stock photos, no photos from someone else's job. If a customer recognizes a stock photo, you've lost them.
  • Protect your customers. No house numbers, license plates, faces or kids without permission. Ask before you post anything from inside a home.
  • The specs: JPG or PNG, between 10 KB and 5 MB. Google recommends 720 × 720 pixels or more. Any recent phone is fine.
  • Keep them sharp and well lit. A blurry photo hurts more than no photo.
The geotag myth

You'll hear that adding GPS "geotags" to your photos boosts rankings, and people sell tools for it. Google's search team has said it doesn't use photo metadata (where the GPS tag lives) for ranking, and photos uploaded to Google are widely reported to have that data stripped. Tests that claim an effect show small, mixed results. Don't pay for geotagging. Spend that time taking better photos.

  • Do today
  • My cover and logo are set, and real.
  • I uploaded 3 real job photos from this week.
  • I've removed any stock or AI photos.
  • Taking "after" photos is now part of packing up.
11

Google Posts

Posts are short updates that show on your profile. Think of them as your shop window: they help a customer who's already looking at you decide to call. Google doesn't say posts help you rank, so don't post for rankings. Post for clicks and trust.

What you can post

  • Update: news, a job spotlight or a tip, with a photo and a button (Call now, Learn more, Book).
  • Offer: a real promotion, with a title and start and end dates.
  • Event: something with a date and time.

Posts are archived after six months unless you set a date range, and they only show once they're marked Live. Google rejects posts with phone numbers it can't connect to your business, misspellings, or auto-generated filler.

Cadence

One post a week, every week. A profile whose newest post is from last spring looks like no one's home.

Six templates to rotate

1. Job spotlight (with before/after photo)

Just finished a [job] in [town]. [One sentence on the problem]. [One sentence on what we did]. If your [thing] is doing the same, give us a call and we'll take a look. [Button: Call now]

2. Seasonal tip

[Season] is coming. Here's a 2-minute check you can do yourself: [simple tip]. If you see [warning sign], that's when to call a pro. [Button: Learn more → your service page]

3. Common question

"[Question customers ask you every week]?" Short answer: [plain answer]. Every home is different, so if you want a straight answer for yours, give us a call. [Button: Call now]

4. Meet the team (with permission)

Meet [first name], who's been with us [time]. If you've had [service] done by us lately, there's a good chance [he/she] was the one at your door. [Button: Book]

5. Real offer (Offer post, only if it's real)

[Offer, e.g. $X off a maintenance visit] through [end date]. Mention this post when you call. [Terms in one line.] [Button: Call now]

6. Service focus

Did you know we also do [service]? We [what's included], for homes in [area]. [One true reason to pick you.] [Button: Learn more → that service's page]

Questions & answers: what's current

Profiles used to have a public "Questions & answers" section where anyone could ask and anyone could answer. Google has been retiring it, and its tool for managing Q&A has been shut down. In its place, Google Maps has been adding AI answers that draw on what's already out there about you: your profile, your reviews and your website.

Because Google rolled this out in stages, check your own profile logged out:

  • Still see old questions? Read them. If an answer is wrong (yours or a stranger's), fix or delete what you can and correct the facts elsewhere on your profile.
  • See no Q&A at all? Nothing to do there. Put your effort into the places below.

Where your FAQ answers go now

  • Services and descriptions (chapter 5): "Do you do tankless?" answered by listing it.
  • Attributes (chapter 9): payments, online estimates, languages.
  • Hours (chapter 8): "Are you open Sunday?"
  • Posts (chapter 11): template 3 answers one common question a month.
  • An FAQ page on your website, in plain words.
  • Do today
  • I checked whether my profile still shows Q&A, and fixed any wrong answers.
  • The 5 questions customers ask most are answered on my profile or website.
13

Chat and booking links

Chat (the Message button)

Google lets verified businesses add a chat option: WhatsApp or text message. When it's set up, a Message button can show on your profile, including next to Call on Google Maps. It's available in select regions, so you may not see it yet.

  1. Open your profile → Edit profile → Contact.
  2. Next to Chat, choose Edit, then WhatsApp or Text message.
  3. For text, enter a number that can receive texts. For WhatsApp, enter your WhatsApp click-to-chat link.
  4. Save, then send yourself a test message from someone else's phone.

If you add both, Google shows customers only the text option.

Only turn it on if someone answers

A Message button that nobody answers is worse than none. The customer waits, gives up and calls the next business. Turn it on only if texts to that number reach a person who replies within minutes during your hours. Many business landlines can't receive texts at all, so test it.

Booking and quote links

Some categories get a booking or appointment link field, and some booking software connects to Google directly.

  • Point it at your own website's quote or booking page, or at booking software in your name. Not at a directory or lead site that sells your customer to others.
  • Click it once a month. Booking links have been hijacked to point at competitors.
  • Google's Performance report only counts bookings made through a connected booking provider. A plain link shows up as a website-style click, if at all.
  • Do today
  • Chat is either off, or on with a number someone answers fast. I tested it.
  • My booking link goes to my own page, and I clicked it to check.

Part 3 · Reviews

14

Getting reviews

Google says more reviews and good ratings help local ranking, and customers compare counts side by side. Most of your happy customers never review because nobody asked them at the right moment in an easy way. Here's the whole system.

1. Get your direct link and QR code

  1. Logged in as the owner, search your business name on Google or open business.google.com.
  2. Look for Ask for reviews (sometimes Get more reviews or Share review form).
  3. Copy the short link. Google also offers a QR code you can download.
  4. Save the link in your phone as a note or a keyboard text shortcut, so it's two taps away on every job.

Put the QR code on a leave-behind card, your invoices and estimates. Our free Review Booster Kit makes a printable card with your own QR code.

2. Ask at the "wow" moment

The best time is right when they see the finished work: the hot water's back, the floor's done, the panel finally works. Ask in person before you leave, then send the text the same day.

Say this (about 30 seconds)

"I'm really glad it came out the way you wanted. Can I ask a favor? Reviews are how a small business like mine gets found. Would you leave me a quick Google review? I'll text you the link right now so it takes a minute."

Then text this

Hi [Name], it's [Your name] from [Business]. Thanks again for having us out today! If you have a minute, a Google review would mean a lot to a small business like ours: [your review link]. Thank you!

One reminder after 3 days, then stop

Hi [Name], just a quick nudge in case the link got buried: [link]. No worries if you're busy. Thanks again for choosing [Business]!

Let them use their own phone, on their own connection. Don't hand them your tablet or have them join your Wi-Fi. Lots of reviews from one device or connection look like one person with many accounts, and Google's filter can pull them.

3. Steady pace

  • Ask every finished job, every week. A few new reviews a week, every week, looks like normal customer behavior.
  • No bursts. Zero for months, then 25 in a weekend, is a pattern Google's rules flag. If you want to ask past customers, spread it over weeks, a few a day.
  • If Google ever pauses new reviews on your profile, hold your asks until it lifts.

4. The rules that keep your reviews up

✓ Allowed

  • Asking every customer for an honest review
  • Sending your direct link by text or email
  • A QR code on a card or invoice
  • A customer naming your tech on their own
  • One friendly reminder

✕ Gets reviews removed (or worse)

  • Incentives: discounts, gift cards, free service, raffle entries, even for "honest" reviews
  • Gating: asking only happy customers, or a "How did we do?" screen that sends only 4 and 5 stars to Google
  • Conflict of interest: reviews from you, your staff, ex-staff, family or friends who never hired you
  • Specific content: telling customers what to write
  • Bought or swapped reviews
The staff-name rule

Google's policy now names two things merchants can't do: ask staff to collect a certain number of reviews, or ask staff to collect reviews that include specific content. That's widely read as covering "please mention Mike by name" and review quotas for techs. So: no quotas, no "mention your tech" in your script, and don't tie techs' pay to review counts. A customer who names your tech on their own is fine.

The FTC rule

The FTC's Consumer Review rule (16 CFR Part 465) bans businesses from writing, buying or selling fake reviews, from giving incentives that depend on a review being positive or negative, from undisclosed reviews by owners, managers or staff, and from using threats to get real reviews taken down. The FTC can seek civil penalties for each violation. Google's own rule is stricter: no incentives of any kind, even for honest reviews.

Check your review software

If you use a review tool, look for a "How was your visit?" screen that sends unhappy customers to a private form. That's gating. Turn that setting off.

More on this in our guides Your Google reviews disappeared? and reviews that stick.

  • My review link is saved in my phone.
  • I texted it to every customer from today.
  • No discounts, drawings, quotas or staff-name asks anywhere.
  • Any review tool I use asks everyone the same way.
15

Replying to every review

Your reply isn't really for the reviewer. It's for the next homeowner scrolling your reviews, deciding whether to call. They want to see a real, calm person who cares about the work.

The rules

  • Reply to every review, good and bad, within 2 days.
  • Write as the business, in the first person: "I" and "we". Never "the company" or "they".
  • Use their first name and mention the type of job. Never their address or private details.
  • Short and specific. 2 to 4 sentences. Change a few words every time so they don't all read the same.
  • No promotions and no stuffing your town and service into every reply.
  • No links or phone numbers. Your number is already on your profile, so "call the office and ask for me" is enough.
  • Never argue in public, never accuse, never admit legal fault. Be honest about real mistakes and take the details offline.
How we write replies

We write review replies for clients every week, always in the business's own first-person voice.

Templates by star rating and situation

5 stars, with details

Thank you, [Name]! [Detail from their review, e.g. "That old water heater put up a fight"], and I'm glad you've got hot water again. We appreciate you trusting us with it.

5 stars, short

Thanks so much, [Name]. It was a pleasure working on your [job]. Enjoy it!

5 stars, no written review (stars only)

Thank you for the 5 stars, [Name]. We really appreciate it.

5 stars, they named your tech

Thank you, [Name]! I'll make sure [tech's first name] sees this. We're glad the [job] went smoothly.

4 stars, small gripe

3 stars, mixed

1–2 stars, fair complaint

1–2 stars, you see it differently

Damage, safety or legal claim (keep it short)

No record of this customer

When to slow down

For anything about damage, injury, safety, a refund fight or a legal threat, keep the public reply to the short template above and talk to your insurer or lawyer before you say more. If a review threatens you or your team, don't reply at all. Flag it (chapter 16).

Spam and fake reviews

A fake review on your profile

  1. Check your records first. Plenty of "never met them" reviews turn out to be a spouse, a tenant, or someone who booked under another name.
  2. Flag it. On Google Maps, tap the three dots on the review → Report review, or use Google's Reviews Management Tool from your profile. Pick the reason that fits: conflict of interest, off topic, spam, harassment and so on.
  3. Reply calmly with the "no record" template. Never call it fake or name who you think wrote it.
  4. Follow up in the Reviews Management Tool. If Google leaves it up, you can appeal once there.
  5. A wave of them, or a demand for money? That's an attack, and it has its own steps, including Google's extortion report. Read Fake review attack: what to do in the first hour.
Why we check every day

We watch our clients' review counts every day, so a wave of fake reviews, or real ones going missing, gets noticed the same day.

Real reviews gone missing? That's usually Google's filter, not spam. See Your Google reviews disappeared?

Spammy competitors

Fake listings, keyword-stuffed names ("Best Cheap Plumber Port City 24/7") and businesses at mailbox addresses take spots on the map that real businesses should have.

  • A wrong name or fake address: on their Maps listing, tap Suggest an edit and correct it, or report it as not a real business. Stick to facts you can see.
  • Persistent or large-scale spam: Google has a Business Redressal Complaint Form for serious cases. Include screenshots and plain evidence.
  • Fight with facts, not revenge. Never leave fake reviews on a competitor. It breaks Google's rules and the FTC's.

17

Weekly, monthly, quarterly routines

Setting the profile up is the easy part. What moves you is doing the same small things every week, for months. Here's the whole job.

Every job

  • Took "after" photos (and "before" if I remembered).
  • Asked for the review in person and texted the link before I left.
  • Sent one reminder 3 days later to anyone who didn't review.

Every week (about 30 minutes)

  • Replied to every new review, in my own voice.
  • Posted one Google post with a real photo.
  • Uploaded a few new photos from this week's jobs.
  • Looked at my own profile on my phone, logged out: name, phone, hours, nothing "closed".
  • Screenshotted my review count.
  • Answered any messages (if chat is on).

Every month (about 30 minutes)

  • Filled in the scoreboard in chapter 18: calls, website clicks, directions, messages.
  • Counted new reviews this month vs the top 3 near me.
  • Tapped my website link, booking link and phone number from the profile.
  • Set special hours for any holiday coming up.
  • Checked for suggested edits from strangers, and fixed anything wrong.

Every quarter (about an hour)

  • Searched logged out again. Who's in the top 3 now, and what categories do they use?
  • Reviewed my extra categories and Services list. Added anything new we do, removed anything we stopped.
  • Refreshed the description if anything changed (new service, new town, new license).
  • Checked People and access. Removed anyone who shouldn't be there.
  • Checked attributes, and swapped the cover photo for a newer real job.
  • Made sure my website, Facebook, Yelp and directories still match my name, phone and hours.
Make it stick

Put it on the calendar: reviews and the post every Monday, the scoreboard on the 1st, the quarterly check in the first week of each season. If it's not on the calendar, it doesn't happen.
