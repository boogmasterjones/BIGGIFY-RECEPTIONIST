01

Social's real job for a trade business

Nobody hires a plumber because of a clever post. But plenty of people decide not to call one because his Facebook page looks dead, or shows a different phone number, or has a three-year-old post and nothing since.

Here's how it usually goes. Someone finds you on Google, or a neighbor drops your name in a group. Before they call, they check you out. They tap your Facebook page or Instagram to answer one question: is this a real, working business I can trust in my house?

So your social pages have two jobs:

  1. Prove you're real and active to people checking you out.Recent job photos, real faces, a truck with your name on it, replies to comments. That's the whole game.
  2. Send the same signals everywhere.Your name, phone, hours and website the same on Facebook, Instagram, Google and your own site, so customers (and Google) never see conflicting info.
The honest truth about Google

Google has never said Facebook or Instagram posts raise your ranking on Maps. If someone sells you social posting "for SEO" or "to rank on Google", walk away. What social does do: it backs up what your Google profile says, it's where people go to double-check you, and wrong or old info on your pages can lead Google and customers to the wrong phone or hours.

That changes what "good" looks like. You don't need viral posts or thousands of followers. You need a page that, in five seconds of scrolling, says: real people, real work, recent, easy to reach.

Do this today: open your own Facebook page on your phone, logged out, and scroll for five seconds. Would you call you? Keep that answer in mind for the rest of this playbook.

03

What to post: the 12-post rotation

"What do I post?" is why most trade pages go quiet. Fix it once: go down this list, one idea per post, then start again at the top. At two posts a week it takes six weeks to come round, and nobody notices the repeat because the jobs are different every time.

PostWhat to shoot or showWhy it works
1. A job photoOne clean, sharp photo of finished work. Tidy the area first.Proof you do the work, recently, near them.
2. Before and afterTwo photos from the same spot, same angle. Take the "before" before you touch anything.The most convincing post a trade can make.
3. The crewYou or your team, first names, on the job or by the truck. Ask each person first.People want to know who's coming to their house.
4. The truckYour lettered truck or van, parked at a job (no house numbers in shot).Says "real, established, local". Repeats your name and number.

04

Photo rules

One blurry or badly cropped photo makes a careful business look careless. These rules take seconds and keep every post looking like you.

✓Do

  • Real photos only, from your own jobs
  • Hold the phone with two hands, elbows in, and tap the screen to focus before you shoot
  • Wipe the lens on your shirt first. Pocket lint is the number one cause of "foggy" photos
  • Shoot in daylight or turn the lights on. Step back so the whole job is in frame
  • Take 3 or 4 shots and keep the sharpest one

✕Don't

  • No AI images, no stock photos, no photos "borrowed" from other companies
  • No house numbers, street signs, license plates, family photos on the fridge or mail on the counter
  • No customer faces or homes without their OK
  • No children unless their family says yes
  • No filters that change what the work really looks like

The crop check (10 seconds, before every post)

  1. Zoom in on the photo.Is the work sharp at full size? If the edges of the tile or the fittings look soft, pick another.
  2. Check the preview.Instagram and Facebook crop photos in the feed. Make sure the important part isn't cut off at the top or sides.
  3. Check the corners.Nothing private, messy or embarrassing in the background.
W
Why we're strict about thisWyatt, Biggify

A client once asked us to pause posts because some photos were blurry or badly cropped. Now every photo is checked before it goes out.

Asking the customer (copy and paste)

Ask before you shoot inside someone's home. Most people say yes, and they like being asked.

In person

"Mind if I grab a couple of photos of the finished job for our page? No house number, no faces, just the work."

By text, after the job

"Hi [Name], it's [Your name] from [Business]. The [job] came out great. OK if I share a photo of it on our Facebook page? Just the work, nothing that shows your address. Totally fine if not."

Kids

Never post a child unless the family has clearly said yes, and even then, never with their name or where they live. When in doubt, crop them out.

05

Caption templates, in your own voice

Write like you talk to a customer at the door. First person, short, plain. One idea per post, and tell people how to reach you. Fill in the brackets and post.

1. Job photo

Wrapped up a [job] in [town] today. [One detail: "Old one was leaking at the base" / "Matched the grout to the original"]. If yours is [doing the same thing], call or text me at [phone].

2. Before and after

Before and after from [town] this week. [What was wrong] → [what we did]. Took us [time]. Swipe to see the before.

3. The crew

This is [Name]. [He/She] has been with us [time] and is the one you'll probably see at your door for [type of job]. [One true human detail: "Best at explaining what went wrong"].

4. The truck

If you see this truck in [town], wave. We're out [days] across [area]. Same number on the side: [phone].

5. A tip

Quick one: [tip in one sentence]. It takes [time] and can save you a call-out. If it [still does X], that's when to call me.

6. Review screenshot

Thank you, [first name]! This is why we [do the thing you're proud of]. [Optional: "Reviews like this keep a small business going."]

7. Seasonal reminder

[Season] is coming. If your [thing] hasn't been [checked/cleaned/serviced] since [last season], now's the time, before everyone calls at once. Call or text [phone].

8. Behind the scenes

[Time] on a [day]. [What's happening: "Loading up for three installs in North Port"]. This is the part nobody sees.

9. FAQ answer

"[The question]" I get asked this on almost every call. Straight answer: [answer]. Anything else you want to know, send me a message.

10. Community

Proud to [sponsor / help out at / be part of] [team or event] this [season]. [Town] has been good to us. Good luck, [team]!

11. New service

New: we now do [service] in [area]. [Why: "A lot of you asked"]. Same crew, same way of working. Message me with questions.

12. Walk-through video

Quick walk-through of a [job] we finished in [town]. [One line on the hardest part]. Watch to the end for the finished [thing].

Three caption rules
  • Only true claims. Don't say "licensed", "best in town" or "same-day" unless it's true, and don't promise prices.
  • Keep hashtags to a few, mostly your town and trade (#PortCharlottePlumber). They matter little. Your photo matters a lot.
  • Different words on each platform is nice, not required. A shorter version for Google posts, the full one for Facebook.
06

How often to post

1–3posts a week is plenty for a trade business.
Every weekmatters more than how many. Steady beats bursts.
  • Busy season: 1 a week. You have the photos, not the time. One post keeps the page alive.
  • Slow season: 2 to 3 a week. You have time, and seasonal reminders and tips bring in the calls you need.
  • Same post, three places: schedule it to Facebook and Instagram together, and put the same photo on Google as an update.
  • Never post just to post. A week with one great job photo beats a week with three filler quotes.

A page with one real job photo every week for a year looks more alive than one with ten posts in January and nothing since.

08

Replying to comments and messages

A message on your page is a customer with their hand up. Answer it like a phone call: fast, friendly, and steer it toward a booking. The faster you reply, the more likely you're still the one they hire.

Scripts to copy

"How much for...?" (in the comments)

Thanks for asking, [Name]! It depends on [the main thing it depends on]. I've sent you a message so I can give you a real number.

"How much for...?" (in messages)

Hi [Name], thanks for reaching out. Most [jobs like this] run between [range] depending on [factor]. If you send me a photo of [the thing], I can narrow it down. Or call/text me at [phone].

"Do you work in [town]?"

Yes, we're in [town] most weeks! What do you need done? Happy to take a look.

(If not: "We don't get out to [town], sorry. [Name of someone you trust] is good out that way.")

A nice comment or a tag

Thank you, [Name]! It was a pleasure working on your [job]. Give us a shout anytime.

You replied late

Hi [Name], sorry for the slow reply, it's been a busy week of jobs. Are you still looking for help with [job]? I can [call you today / come out on day].

A message that's really a booking

Got it, thanks [Name]. What's the address and the best number to reach you? I have [day] at [time] or [day] at [time]. Which works better?

Save time

Meta Business Suite lets you save replies and set an instant reply for new messages. Set one: "Thanks for messaging [Business]! I'm probably on a job. I'll get back to you today. If it's urgent, call or text [phone]." Then make sure you really do.

Never move the money talk or personal details into public comments. Prices, addresses and phone numbers go in messages or on the phone.

09

A bad comment in public

Everyone reading is judging you on the reply, not the complaint. A calm, short answer makes you look like the business they'd want if something went wrong at their house.

  1. Wait an hour, not a week.Don't reply angry. But don't leave it for days, either. Reply the same day.
  2. Check who it is.Look them up in your jobs. Real customer, someone who never hired you, or spam?
  3. Reply once, in public, short.Thank them, own what's fair, and move it to the phone. No details of the job, no blame, no arguing.
  4. Call or message them privatelyand try to fix it.
  5. If it gets sorted,a short follow-up comment ("Glad we got this sorted, thanks [Name]") shows everyone how it ended.
A real customer, unhappy

[Name], I'm sorry you're not happy with how this went. That's not how we want anyone to feel. I'd like to make it right. I'm sending you a message now, or call me directly at [phone]. [Your name], owner.

Someone you can't find as a customer

Hi [Name], I take every complaint seriously, but I can't find a job under this name. Could you message me with the address or date so I can look into it? [Your name], owner.

✓Hide or remove only

  • Spam and scam links
  • Threats, slurs, harassment
  • Someone's private info (a phone number, an address)

✕Don't delete

  • An honest complaint, even an unfair-sounding one
  • A comment just because it's embarrassing
  • Then pretend it never happened. People screenshot, and a deleted complaint comes back louder

On Facebook, "Hide" keeps a comment visible only to the person who wrote it and their friends. Use it for spam, not criticism. For a bad Google review, the steps are different: see fake review attacks and reviews that disappeared.

10

Facebook groups: help first, never spam

Local groups (your town's neighbors group, the parents' group, the buy-and-sell) are where people ask "Anyone know a good electrician?" Being the helpful one there is worth more than any post on your own page.

✓Do

  • Read the group rules first. Many only allow business posts on one day, or not at all
  • Answer questions with real advice, even when it means they don't need you
  • Say who you are: "I own [Business], so I'm biased, but..."
  • Comment from your personal profile like a neighbor, unless the group allows Pages
  • Thank people who recommend you

✕Don't

  • Paste the same ad into ten groups
  • Drop your link under every question, even off-topic ones
  • Have friends or family "recommend" you without saying they know you
  • Make fake accounts to praise yourself. It's deceptive and admins spot it
  • Argue with competitors in the comments
Someone asks for a recommendation in your trade

I own [Business], so I'm biased! But happy to help. [One useful line: "If it's only the breaker tripping, try X first"]. If you need someone out, I'm at [phone].

A general question you can answer

Not a sales pitch, just from doing this [years] years: [the answer]. Hope that helps!

The rule of thumb: for every time you mention your business, answer two or three questions where you don't. People remember the helpful one.

11

Short videos, made simply

You don't need editing software, a mic or a script. A steady 30-second clip of real work is more convincing than anything polished.

  1. Hold the phone upright (vertical).That's the shape Reels and phone screens use.
  2. Keep it steady.Two hands, elbows tucked, move slowly. Or lean the phone on something, or use a cheap phone clamp.
  3. Keep it short.15 to 45 seconds. Start on the problem or the finished job, not on you saying hello.
  4. Talk like you're showing a customer,or don't talk at all and add a line of text on screen.
  5. Turn on captions.Most people watch with the sound off. The apps can usually add them for you; check them for mistakes.
  6. Post it as a Reelon Instagram and Facebook together, and pick a clear cover frame of the finished work.

Five videos any trade can make this month

  • The walk-through: walk slowly through a finished job, pointing out one detail.
  • The 3-second before/after: film the before, then the same angle after.
  • "What I found": the corroded part, the burnt wire, the rotted board, and one sentence on what causes it.
  • The quick tip: 20 seconds on something homeowners can check themselves.
  • Meet the crew: each person says their name and how long they've done this. With their OK.
Music and privacy

Business accounts get a smaller music library. Use only sounds the app offers you, never a song you added from elsewhere. And the same photo rules apply: no house numbers, no faces or kids without a yes.

13

What to measure: calls and messages, not likes

A post with 200 likes and no calls did less for you than a post with 6 likes that got one message about a water heater. Count what pays.

The three numbers that matter

  1. Messages from Facebook and Instagramthat turned into a quote or a job.
  2. Calls where the customer said "I saw you on Facebook" or "Instagram".Ask on every call: "How did you hear about us?" and write it down.
  3. Taps on your call button and website link.Your Facebook Page and Instagram dashboards show these where available, and Google's "Performance" view shows calls and website clicks from your profile.

After a month, look back: which posts brought messages? Usually it's before/afters, walk-through videos and seasonal reminders. Do more of whatever worked for you.

Remember the first job, too: some people will check your page and call without ever liking or messaging. That's why "How did you hear about us?" matters so much.

15

Quick answers

Do Facebook posts help me rank on Google Maps?

Google has never said that Facebook or Instagram posts raise your Maps ranking, so don't pay anyone who promises it. Social helps in other ways: people check you out there before they call, and your name, phone and hours matching everywhere keeps Google from showing wrong information.

How often should I post?

One to three times a week is plenty. Steady beats busy: a page with a real job photo every week looks more alive than one with ten posts in a burst and then nothing for two months.

Can I use AI images or stock photos?

No. The point of posting is proving you're real. Customers spot fake and stock photos, and a fake photo undoes the trust the rest of your page builds. Post real photos from your own jobs.

Should I delete a bad comment?

Not if it's an honest complaint. Reply once, calmly, and take it to a phone call. Hide or remove only spam, threats, slurs or someone's private information.
