Fake competitor listings on Google Maps: what actually gets them removed

"Plumber Near Me 24/7 Emergency" sits above you on Maps with an address that's an empty lot. You reported it. Nothing happened. Most owners say the same thing: Google ignores my reports. Usually the report went through the wrong door with no proof. Here's the right door for each kind of fake, and what to put in the envelope.

14 min read Checked against Google's current rules By Wyatt, Biggify

You'll walk away with

  • The 6 patterns that give a fake listing away
  • Which Google tool to use for which problem, and the proof that goes with it
  • A copy-paste complaint and a spam log you can start today

The 20-second version

  2. Small, fixable lies (keywords stuffed in a real business's name): use Suggest an edit on Maps.

01

Why your reports get ignored

Google removes huge numbers of fake profiles every year, most of them before anyone sees them. The ones that get through are the ones good enough to fool the automation. So a bare "this is fake" click rarely moves them.

What tends to work, according to the local-search experts who fight this every day and Google's own form instructions:

  • The right tool for the problem. A Maps edit is mostly judged by machines. The redressal form is reviewed by people.
  • Proof a reviewer can check fast. A Maps link, a Street View link, a license search showing nothing. Not "trust me, I know this market."
  • One specific claim per listing. "The name has 'Emergency Water Heater' added that isn't on their sign" beats "this whole company is a scam."
  • Persistence with a paper trail. Many fakes take more than one round.
Be straight with yourself

None of this is a sure thing. Google says outright that it can't promise action on a complaint and won't send you status updates. What follows is how to give your report its best shot.

02

Fix your own house first

Before you report anyone, make sure your profile would pass the same check. Reporting puts your category and your town under a microscope. Spammers also report back.

  • Your name matches your sign, truck and invoices. No added city or service.
  • Your address is real. Work from home? Real home address, hidden, with service areas set. No mailbox store or virtual office.
  • One profile per business, no extras for "drain cleaning" or the next town over.

Not sure? Our guide on changing your Google profile safely covers each one, and how to fix it without getting suspended.

03

How to spot a fake

Search your main service the way a customer would, on your phone, in a few different neighborhoods. Then look at every listing above you for these patterns.

1. Keyword-stuffed names

"ABC Plumbing Water Heater Repair Port Charlotte". Google's rules say the name must be the business's real-world name, with no service descriptions, locations or taglines added. Check their website, truck photos and Facebook. If the real name is "ABC Plumbing", the rest is stuffing.

2. Fake or mailbox addresses

Drop into Street View at the pin. Common giveaways: an empty lot, a home with no sign of the business, a UPS Store or other mailbox shop, a shared "virtual office" building, a suite number that doesn't exist. Google's rules say a mailbox or virtual office you don't actually staff can't have a profile.

3. Lead-gen networks

The classic ones are locksmiths, garage door repair, plumbers, water damage and appliance repair, but every trade sees them. Signs you're looking at one:

  • Generic names: "Garage Door Repair [Town]", "24 Hour Locksmith Pros".
  • Several listings in a cluster, each a few streets apart, each with a different phone number.
  • No website, or near-identical template websites with only the town name swapped.
  • Whoever answers says "garage door service" or "plumbing", never a company name.
  • No state license under that name, and no business registered with the state.

Google's rules say lead-generation agents aren't eligible for a Business Profile. That's the rule these break.

4. Duplicate listings

The same company with two or three profiles: same phone or website, slightly different names, or one "location" per town with no real office behind it. Google allows one profile per business per real location.

5. Fake reviews

Bursts of 5-star reviews in a few days, reviewers with one review ever, or the same reviewers praising the same handful of businesses across states. Flag each review on its own (more on that below). If someone's hitting your profile with fake 1-stars, that's a different fight: see what to do when you're hit with fake reviews.

6. Hijacked or "closed" real businesses

A real competitor that suddenly shows a different phone number or website link can be a hijack, not them. Be careful here. That owner is a victim too, and a quiet heads-up to them may do more than a report.

04

Suggest an edit, Report, or the form?

Google has three doors. Owners who say "nothing works" have usually only tried the first one.

DoorUse it forWho looks at it
Suggest an edit (on the listing in Maps)Fixing one fact on a real business: a stuffed name, the wrong category. Or marking a place that isn't there through the "close or remove" options.Mostly automated. Fast, but easy to overrule.
Report (the flag on a review or photo)One fake review, or an offensive or fake photo. Pick the reason that fits, like conflict of interest or spam.Mostly automated, review by review.
Business Redressal Complaint FormFraud on a business's name, address, phone or website, or "this business doesn't exist". Networks of fakes.Reviewed by Google staff against its guidelines.

Suggest an edit, step by step

  1. Sign in to Googleon the account you use every day. A real account with a history of normal edits carries more weight.
  2. Open the listing in Google Mapsand tap Suggest an edit.
  3. For a stuffed name:choose to change the name and type the real-world name exactly as it is on their website or sign.
  4. For a place that isn't there:use the close or remove option and pick the reason that fits, such as "doesn't exist here" or the spam or fake reason. The exact wording moves around between app versions.
  5. Add a photo if it offers(their real sign or truck, or the empty lot) and submit. Note it in your log.
Start small, then escalate

Try Suggest an edit first on stuffed names. It costs two minutes. If it's rejected or flips back within a couple of weeks, move to the redressal form with your evidence.

The reviewer has minutes per complaint. Hand them proof they can click.

Where to find the proof

  • Street View: on the listing, drag the little person onto the pin. If the imagery shows several capture dates, check them. Copy the link from your browser.
  • State license lookup: search "[your state] contractor license lookup" or the board for the trade (plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and locksmiths in states that license them). Search the business name and any owner names.
  • Secretary of State business search: every state has one (Florida's is Sunbiz). A company with no registration, or a registration in another state with a mail-drop address, is worth noting.
  • The website itself: no license number, no real address, photos you can find on a dozen other sites. A reverse image search on their "team" photo can be telling.
06

Filing the redressal form

The form is called the Business Redressal Complaint Form, at support.google.com/business/contact/business_redressal_form. Google says it's for misleading or fraudulent info on a business's name, phone number or website on Maps. It's not for correcting an honest mistake. Use Suggest an edit for that.

  1. Sign inwith your normal Google account.
  2. Fill in your name and email.You can name your own business as the one affected.
  3. Pick the content type:title, address, phone number, website, or "This business doesn't exist".
  4. Paste the Maps URL.It has to be the full link starting with https://www.google.com/maps, not a shortened share link. On a phone, open the listing in a browser to get it.
  5. Upload your evidenceif there's a file option: one PDF with your screenshots is easiest.
  6. Write the explanation.Quote the exact wrong content, then state the fact and the link that proves it. Use the template below.
  7. Save the confirmationor any case number to your log.

Copy-paste explanation

Edit the brackets, keep it this short

The listing shows the business name "[exact name on Maps]". Their website [link] and their truck [photo link] show the real name is "[real name]". The added words "[stuffed words]" are service and location keywords, which the guidelines for representing your business on Google don't allow in a business name.

For a fake location: The listing places this business at [address]. Street View [link] shows [an empty lot / a private home with no signage / a mailbox store]. No license is on file with [state board] [link to search] and no company by this name is registered with the [state] Secretary of State [link]. We believe this business doesn't operate at this address.

  • Stick to facts. Leave out "scammers", "ripping people off", or how much business they cost you.
  • Group a network in one complaint where the form allows several URLs, and say what ties them together (same phone, same website template, same reviewers).
  • Don't call a real business fake because one field is wrong. Report the field. Overclaiming weakens the whole complaint.
07

Calling the fake (and the recording trap)

Calling the number on a suspicious listing often tells you everything: who answers, what name they give, where the "local" tech actually comes from. Write down what you heard in your log right after the call.

Don't record without consent

Florida, California, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Washington and several other states require everyone on a call to agree before it's recorded. When the two people are in different states, play it safe and follow the stricter rule. Recording a call illegally can be a crime. Notes are fine. A recording is only worth having if everyone consented. If you're unsure, ask a lawyer before you record anything.

  • Don't book a fake job or waste anyone's time. One short call answering "what's the company name and where are you based?" is plenty.
  • Don't pretend to be Google, a cop or an inspector.
08

Escalating to Product Experts

If the form doesn't work, the next step is the Google Business Profile Help Community at support.google.com/business/community. The people who answer there are Product Experts: volunteers, not Google employees. They can't remove anything themselves. The senior ones can pass well-documented cases to Google staff.

  1. File the redressal form first.Experts will usually ask whether you have, and for any case ID.
  2. Write a clear title:"[Trade] spam network in [town]: [number] fake listings, redressal already filed".
  3. In the post:the Maps links, the pattern in one or two sentences, what you filed and when, and the proof as links (Street View, license search). Experts point out that links can be checked fast where screenshots can't.
  4. Be patient and polite.Threads can get marked as duplicates or go quiet. Reply with any new evidence instead of starting over.

Other spam fighters gather in independent forums like Local Search Forum. Those can be useful for advice, but they aren't run by Google.

09

How long it takes, and why they come back

DaysAn edit on a stuffed name can go live, or get rejected, quickly.
WeeksRedressal complaints often take longer, and you won't get an update. Re-check the listing yourself.

Why fakes come back:

  • The owner of the listing can edit the name back. A stuffed name you fixed can be re-stuffed next week.
  • Networks rebuild. When one listing goes, a new one can appear with a new phone number and a new address.
  • Reinstatement. A removed listing can be appealed and sometimes comes back.

That's why the owners who make progress treat it as a monthly chore, not a one-time fight. Twenty minutes a month: check the listings above you, re-file what's back, add new proof.

The other half of the fight

Spam removal helps. It doesn't replace being the best real choice. More recent reviews, real job photos, a website with a page for each service and town, and picking up the phone when the fake can't. That's what keeps you ahead when the next fake shows up.

10

Keep a spam log

A spreadsheet or a notebook page. It turns "I've reported them a hundred times" into dated evidence you can hand to Google, a Product Expert or a licensing board. Use one row per listing per report:

ColumnWhat goes in it
ListingName as shown and the full Maps URL
ProblemStuffed name, fake address, doesn't exist, duplicate, fake reviews
ProofLinks: Street View, license search, state registration, their website. Where the screenshots are saved.
FiledWhen, and how: edit, review flag, redressal form, community thread
Case / threadAny case ID, confirmation or thread link
ResultRemoved, fixed, rejected, came back (and when you re-checked)

Take your screenshots the day you find the problem. A spammer who sees reports coming can tidy up, and then your proof is gone.

12

Quick answers

Why does Google ignore my reports?

Usually because the report has no proof Google can check, or it went through the wrong tool. A quick Maps edit is judged mostly by automation. Fraud on a name, phone number or website, or a business that doesn't exist, belongs on the Business Redressal Complaint Form, with links a person can verify in a few minutes.

Can I get in trouble for reporting a competitor?

Not for honest, factual reports. Stick to what you can prove, don't use fake accounts to pile on edits, and don't record calls without the consent your state requires. Many states, including Florida and California, need everyone on the call to agree.

How long does removal take?

There's no set time. Google says it can't promise action and won't send status updates on redressal complaints. Some fakes go in days, some take weeks and several rounds of evidence, and some never go.

Should I pay someone to remove spam listings?

Every tool they use is free and in this guide. If you do hire help, pay for time and reporting, not for promised removals. Nobody outside Google can promise a removal.
