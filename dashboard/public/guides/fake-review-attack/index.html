Hit with a wave of fake 1-star reviews? What to do in the first hour (and the first week)

You wake up to a pile of 1-stars from people you've never met. Maybe there's a message saying "pay us and they stop". Maybe there's nothing at all, which is more common. Either way, there's a right order to do things in, and the first hour matters most.

14 min read Checked against Google's current rules By Wyatt, Biggify

You'll walk away with

  • A first-hour plan you can run from your phone
  • Which flag reason to pick for each kind of fake review
  • 4 calm reply templates you can copy today

The 20-second version

Jump to the first hour →
  1. Don't pay and don't reply to anyone demanding money. Paying tells them you're an easy target.
  2. Screenshot everything now: the messages, every review, every reviewer's profile, and the times.
  3. Got a money demand? Report it on Google's form for "negative review extortion scams". It's separate from flagging.
  4. Flag each fake review with the closest reason, then track it in Google's Reviews Management Tool.
  5. Keep real reviews coming at a steady pace. Don't buy reviews and don't run a big burst to "fight back".

01

The first hour

Don't start typing replies. Collect proof first, because fake reviews and the accounts behind them can vanish, and once they're gone your proof goes with them.

  1. Don't pay. Don't answer the person demanding money.No "how much?", no "who are you?", no threats back. Google's own advice is not to engage, and paying doesn't make the reviews go away.
  2. Screenshot every message.Text, email, WhatsApp, Facebook, a voicemail. Get the sender's number, email or username and the date and time in the shot.
  3. Screenshot every review.The star rating, the words and how long ago it was posted. Copy each review's link too: open the review in Google Maps and tap Share.
  4. Screenshot every reviewer's profile.Tap their name. Note how many reviews they've written, where, and whether the photo or name looks made up. Ten accounts that only ever reviewed you, all from the same night, is a pattern Google can act on.
  5. Write down the timeline.When the 1-stars started, how many came in, and when any message arrived. Two lines in your notes app is enough.
  6. Check who has access to your profile.In your Business Profile: menu → Business Profile settings → People and access. Remove anyone you don't know. Make sure your phone and website haven't been changed.
  7. Tell your team to stay quiet.No replies from staff, no angry Facebook post naming anyone. You'll reply later, once, calmly.
Make one folder

Put every screenshot in one album or folder called "Review attack". You'll attach these to Google's forms, a police report and maybe a lawyer's letter. Finding them in 400 camera-roll photos later is miserable.

02

If they want money ("pay us and the reviews stop")

This is review extortion. It usually goes one of two ways: a pile of 1-stars lands, then a message asks for money, gift cards or crypto to remove them. Or the message comes first: "pay, or the 1-stars start".

Google has a dedicated form for exactly this. It's separate from flagging reviews. Look up Google's help page called "Report negative review extortion scams on your Business Profile", or go straight to support.google.com/business/contact/merchant_extortion. Be signed in to the Google account that manages your profile.

The form asks for:

  • Your contact details and how you're connected to the business
  • Your business name, address and Business Profile link
  • Links to the suspicious reviews
  • How the scammer contacted you and what they asked for: names, usernames, emails, phone numbers
  • Your screenshots, with dates and times showing
  • When the 1-stars started, when the demand came, and any earlier attempts
Don't hire a "review removal" service in a panic

Nobody can make Google delete a review for a fee. Anyone who calls or messages you during an attack offering to "remove" the reviews may be the same people running it. Everything a legit helper does is in this guide, and Google's forms are free.

Google says it investigates and tells you the result, but it won't share the details of what it found. There's no promised timeline, so file it the same day.

03

If there's no message at all

This is the more common case. The 1-stars just appear. No demand, no explanation. The usual causes:

  • A bot attack. Many accounts with foreign or random names, no photos, few or no other reviews, all in a short window. Often a test before a demand arrives, so keep watching your messages and spam folder.
  • One angry person with several accounts. Same writing style, same complaint, posted days apart.
  • A competitor, an ex-employee or someone you know personally. More on these below.
  • A real event. A local Facebook post or news story sends strangers to pile on. Those reviewers aren't customers either, and Google treats them the same way (see below).

No message means no extortion form. Go straight to flagging each review, and keep your screenshots in case a demand shows up later.

04

Which flag reason to pick

Flag each review one by one. From your Business Profile: Read reviews → the flag icon next to the review → pick a reason → Send report. Or use Google's Reviews Management Tool, which also shows the status of everything you've flagged.

The reason matters. A real customer who was rude still gets to post. A review that isn't from a real experience doesn't. Pick the reason that best describes why the review breaks the rules, not just that it's unfair.

Also report the reviewer's profile if it's obviously fake: in Google Maps, tap the reviewer's name, then the menu, then Report profile.

05

What Google does on its own

Google now watches for exactly this kind of attack. When its systems spot a sudden spike in spam reviews on a profile, Google says it will:

Removethe fake reviews it catches, so they don't drag down your rating.
Pausenew reviews and ratings on your profile for a while. The email says it's typically lifted within a few days.
Email youwith a subject like "Protecting your Business Profile from spam reviews". You don't need to do anything.
Tell customerswith a banner on your profile explaining why reviews are paused.
  • A pause is protection, not punishment. Your real reviews stay. You just can't get new ones until it lifts, so don't panic if a happy customer says their review "didn't go through".
  • If real reviews got removed in the sweep, the email says you can ask Google to take another look.
  • Not every attack gets caught. Slow attacks, a few reviews a week, often slip past the filter. That's why you still flag each one yourself.
The other warning is the one you don't want

Google has a second, different notice for businesses it catches with fake reviews: "Suspected fake reviews were recently removed from this place." For those businesses it can also block new reviews and hide existing ones for a set time. Buying 5-stars to bury the 1-stars is how a victim ends up with that one.

06

Following up, and how long it takes

  1. Give it several days.Google says flagged reviews are usually evaluated within several days. Big attacks can take longer.
  2. Check the status in the Reviews Management Tool.Choose "Check the status of a review I reported previously". You'll see Decision pending, Report reviewed - no policy violation, or a removal.
  3. Appeal the ones that stayed up.Each flagged review gets one appeal, from the same tool ("Appeal eligible reviews"). You can select up to 10 at a time. Add the pattern: "one of 30 reviews from new accounts in 6 hours" says more than "this is fake".
  4. Then contact Business Profile support.From the Business Profile help center, choose to contact support about reviews. Have your screenshots, review links and any extortion report reference ready. Stay short and factual.
  5. Still stuck?Post in the Google Business Profile Help Community with your profile link and the pattern. Volunteer Product Experts there can sometimes escalate clear-cut cases.

An appeal that's turned down means Google decided the review doesn't break its rules. Don't keep re-flagging it. Answer it publicly instead (next section) and let new real reviews push it down.

07

Should you reply? Yes, briefly. Here's how.

Your reply isn't for the reviewer. It's for the next homeowner scrolling through your reviews, deciding whether to call. They want to see someone calm and reasonable.

✓Do

  • Write as the business, in first person: "we" and "I"
  • Say you can't find a record of the job
  • Give a real way to reach you
  • Keep it to 2 or 3 sentences
  • Flag first, then reply. A reply doesn't stop Google removing a review

✕Don't

  • Call it fake, a scam, a competitor or a bot in public
  • Name who you think wrote it
  • Share any customer's address, job or details, even to prove a point
  • Threaten to sue
  • Paste the same long essay under 40 reviews
W
How we write repliesWyatt, Biggify

We write review replies for our clients every week, always in the business's own first-person voice: "we", never "they".

Copy these, swap in your details, and change a few words on each one so they don't all read the same.

Template 1: No record of this job

"Thanks for posting. We can't find any record of doing work for you, and we take every review seriously. If we did, please call me at [phone] so I can look into it myself. [First name], owner"

Template 2: The short one, for a burst of reviews

"We don't have a record of this job. If we worked for you, please call us at [phone] and we'll make it right."

Template 3: Probably meant for another company

"Thanks for the feedback. We don't offer [service] and we can't find this job in our records, so this may be meant for another company. If it was us, please call me at [phone] and I'll sort it out."

Template 4: Not about a job at all

"We're sorry you feel this way. This review doesn't describe any work we've done, so we've asked Google to look at it. Anyone with a real concern about a job of ours can reach me directly at [phone]."

When not to reply: if a review threatens you or someone on your team, don't answer it at all. Flag it as harassment and see police below.

08

Never-a-customer cases

Some fake reviews aren't from bots. They're from real people who were never your customers.

Who wrote itWhat to do
A job applicant you didn't hireFlag it. Google's reviews are for customer experiences, and employment ties are a conflict of interest. Reply with Template 1 or 4. Don't mention the interview in public.
An ex-employeeFlag as Conflict of interest. Former employment is named in Google's policy. Never discuss their job, pay or why they left in a reply.
An ex-partner, relative or neighborFlag as Conflict of interest, or Bullying or harassment if it attacks a person. If it's part of a wider pattern of harassment, keep records for a lawyer or the police.
A competitorFlag as Conflict of interest. Never accuse them in public, even if you're sure. If you can prove it (an email, an account linked to their business), talk to a lawyer.
A visit or job that never happenedCheck your job records, invoices and call log first. Then flag as Off topic and reply with Template 1 or 3. Sometimes it's a real customer of a company with a similar name.
Be sure before you call it fake

Look up the name and the address in your system before you decide. Plenty of "I never met this person" reviews turn out to be a spouse, a tenant, or a customer who booked under a different name. A calm "no record" reply covers you either way.

09

Keep the phone ringing meanwhile

The best defense against 20 fake 1-stars is a steady stream of real reviews, before, during and after. But steady is the key word.

  • Ask every finished job, every week. A few new reviews a week, every week, looks natural and keeps your rating moving back up.
  • No sudden burst. Texting your whole customer list in one night can look like the same kind of spike Google is filtering, and real reviews can get caught with the fake ones.
  • Remember the pause. If Google has paused reviews on your profile, hold your asks until it lifts. Otherwise happy customers try, fail and don't try again.
  • Don't buy reviews, don't pay for them, don't offer a discount for them. Google bans incentives for reviews. And see the warning in section 05.
  • Don't only ask happy customers. Asking only the ones you know will give 5 stars ("review gating") is against Google's rules.
  • Answer every call. While your stars are down, the callers you do get matter more. A missed call during an attack is a customer who goes to the next listing.
10

When to involve police, the FBI or the FTC

Police and the FBI: when there's a demand or a threat

  • Anyone threatening violence: call 911.
  • A demand for money to stop the reviews: that's extortion. File a report with your local police (non-emergency line) and get the report number. Add it to your Google extortion report and your notes.
  • The demand came online or from overseas: also file at the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, ic3.gov. Attach the same screenshots.
  • Don't expect an arrest. Many of these crews are overseas. The point is the paper trail, which backs up your case with Google and protects you if it gets worse.

The FTC: what its fake-review rule actually covers

The FTC's Consumer Review rule (16 CFR Part 465) bans businesses from writing, buying or selling fake reviews, from paying for reviews that say something positive or negative, and from bullying people into taking reviews down. The FTC can seek civil penalties for each violation.

  • It's aimed at businesses and the people who sell fake reviews, not at ordinary people who post a review. A stranger leaving you a 1-star isn't who it targets.
  • A competitor who pays for fake 1-stars on you is the kind of business conduct the rule is about. You can report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov. The FTC doesn't handle individual cases for you, but reports help it spot patterns.
  • It cuts both ways. The same rule says you can't use threats or groundless legal threats to scare real customers into deleting reviews, and you can't buy 5-stars to fight back.

A lawyer makes sense when you know who's behind it (a competitor, an ex-employee, an ex-partner) and the damage is real. Google has a separate process for legal removal requests, and a lawyer can file one or get a court order if it comes to that.

Quick answers

Should I pay to make the reviews stop?

No. Google says paying or engaging can encourage more attempts and doesn't make the reviews go away. Screenshot the messages and report them through Google's form for negative review extortion scams.

How long does Google take to remove fake reviews?

Google says a flagged review is usually evaluated within several days. You can check the status, and appeal once, in the Reviews Management Tool. The extortion form has no stated timeline, so report quickly and keep your evidence.

Should I reply to a fake review?

A short, calm reply is fine and is written for future customers, not the reviewer. Say you have no record of the job and give a way to reach you. Never accuse anyone and never share customer details.

Will buying good reviews fix the damage?

No. Google can block new reviews, hide existing ones and show a warning on businesses it catches with fake reviews, and the FTC's Consumer Review rule lets it seek civil penalties from businesses that buy fake reviews. Ask every real customer instead, steadily.
